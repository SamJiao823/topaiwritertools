The short answer: AI is a genuinely good fit for Google Ads copy, because ad writing is constrained rather than creative — fixed character limits, a fixed number of variations, and a clear goal. That is the shape of problem language models handle well. The catch is that Google Ads rewards variety across assets, and AI tends to produce fifteen headlines that are subtle rewrites of the same idea. Use it for volume, then check that your headlines are actually different angles and not synonyms.

Give it limits, not adjectives

The single biggest improvement to AI ad copy is putting the hard constraints in the prompt. "Write headlines" produces unusable text. "Write 15 headlines, each under 30 characters, each making a different type of claim — two about price, two about speed, two about risk reversal, two about the specific outcome, two about who it is for, five about the core offer" produces assets you can actually upload.

Count characters in the output before you trust it. Models are unreliable at exact character counts, and a 31-character headline is not a soft failure — it will not serve.

Pin only when you have a reason

Responsive search ads mix your assets and learn which combinations perform. Pinning headlines to fixed positions stops that learning. The usual advice is to leave assets unpinned unless you have a compliance requirement — a legal disclaimer or a claim that must appear in position one. If you do pin, pin one position, not three, or you reduce the system to a single ad.

Match the copy to the keyword, not the brand

Ad copy that mirrors the search term consistently outperforms copy that mirrors the brand voice. Practically, that means generating per ad group rather than per campaign: give AI the keyword set for one ad group and ask for headlines that repeat that intent in plain words. Ten ad groups with tailored copy beat one campaign with general copy, and generating ten tailored sets is exactly where AI saves real time.

What to keep out of AI output

  • Unverifiable claims. "Number one", "best rated", "clinically proven" — each needs evidence you actually hold, and Google enforces this at the policy level.
  • Prices and availability. These change. Ads with a stale price are a policy problem and a conversion problem.
  • Invented offers. Free trials, guarantees and shipping terms must match the landing page exactly. Mismatch is a disapproval.
  • Trademarked terms. Using competitor brand names in copy has specific rules that vary by region.

A working process

  1. Start from the keyword set, not the product description.
  2. Generate more than you need — 20-30 headlines per ad group, then cut.
  3. Check character counts and deduplicate. Delete headlines that say the same thing differently.
  4. Verify every factual claim against the landing page you are sending traffic to.
  5. Let the data decide. Upload the variations and read the asset performance report rather than arguing about which headline is better.

What do people ask about AI writing tools for Google Ads?

Can AI write Google Ads?

Yes. Ad copy is constrained rather than creative, which suits language models well. Generate more variations than you need, verify character counts and factual claims, then let asset performance data decide what stays.

How do I choose an AI for Google Ads copy?

Claude tends to produce stronger, tighter copy; Rytr is better value when you need many variations across lots of ad groups. Both need the character limits stated explicitly in the prompt.

How many headlines should I generate?

Ask for 20-30 per ad group, then cut to the strongest 10-15. The important part is that they make genuinely different claims rather than being rewrites of one idea.

Should I pin headlines in responsive search ads?

Usually no. Pinning stops the system from learning which combinations perform. Pin only for compliance reasons, and then only one position.
