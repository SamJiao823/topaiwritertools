A $7.50 subscription is not $7.50 worth of writing, and a $200 article is not $200 of your time. The honest comparison has three columns: what you pay in money, what you pay in your own hours, and what you are actually buying — drafts, or judgement. Most people compare only the first column and then wonder why the cheap option feels expensive.

What the subscription actually costs

Take our 30-day benchmark. Two tools, same brief, same editing routine:

Low-cost dedicated writerGeneral assistant
Subscription~$7.50 / month~$20 / month
Editing time per 1,000 words~22 minutes~8 minutes
Your time cost per piece (at $30/hr)~$11~$4
Ten pieces per month, all in~$118~$60

The cheaper subscription costs about twice as much per month once your own editing time is priced in. That is not an argument against it — unlimited generation is genuinely useful when volume, not quality, is the constraint — but it means "$7.50 versus $200" is not the comparison.

What a writer actually costs

Freelance rates vary enormously by subject and by whether they understand your market. Rather than quote numbers we have not verified across your situation, the useful question is what you receive:

  • A first draft you did not write — the part a tool can also do.
  • Judgement about what to write — the part tools cannot do, and usually why experienced writers cost more.
  • Sourcing and verification — interviews, original research, actual figures. This is where the premium is usually justified.
  • No editing burden on you beyond direction and revision rounds.

A cheap freelance article often delivers the first item only, which is exactly what the subscription gives you at a fraction of the price. That is the real trap: paying writer rates for draft-generation work.

Where tools win

  • Formats that repeat. Job descriptions, service reports, follow-up sequences, social captions — anything you will write again next month benefits from a fixed template.
  • Restructuring work you have already written. Turning notes into sections, reordering, tightening. Very high return.
  • Producing variants. Several genuinely different structures so you can choose, before committing to one.
  • Volume where each piece has low stakes. Where being mostly right is fine and being slow is expensive.

Where writers win

  • Anything requiring original reporting. Interviews, first-hand testing, data nobody has published.
  • Regulated or high-liability content. Legal, medical, financial, anything where an invented sentence creates a real problem.
  • Positioning decisions. What to say, to whom, and what to deliberately not say.
  • Anything where your editing time is the real bottleneck. If you do not have fifteen minutes per piece, no subscription solves that.

The hybrid that usually wins

Most teams we watched ended up here, and for predictable reasons: a writer sets the brief and the angle; a tool produces consistent drafts against that brief; the writer edits rather than writes from scratch. Output goes up, cost per piece goes down, and the judgement stays where it should be. It also solves the failure mode of the cheap route — nobody notices when twenty pieces all start sounding the same.

A test worth running

Pick one real document you produce repeatedly. Write it three ways over two weeks: with a tool, by your own hand, and by a writer if the budget exists. Count hours as well as money. The answer usually depends less on which is cheaper overall and more on which removes the bottleneck you actually have — drafts, or decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an AI writing tool cheaper than hiring a writer?

On subscription price, clearly yes. Once you price your own editing time, the gap narrows substantially — in our benchmark the cheap tool cost around twice what the stronger model did per month in total.

Can AI replace a freelance writer?

For repeated formats with established briefs, largely yes. For original reporting, regulated subjects and positioning decisions, no — those are judgement rather than drafting.

What is the biggest hidden cost of AI writing tools?

Editing time. Roughly 22 minutes per 1,000 words on a low-cost tool versus about 8 on a stronger assistant, which at $30 per hour reverses the apparent price difference.

Is the hybrid approach better?

For most teams we watched, yes: a writer sets angle and brief, a tool produces consistent drafts against it, and the writer edits rather than starts from nothing.

When should I stop using a tool for a piece?

When you have read it twice and still cannot tell whether a claim is true. That is the point where the cost moves from editing to verification, which is not what these tools are for.
