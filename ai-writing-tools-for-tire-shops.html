Tyre retail has a particular writing problem: a large share of customer questions carry a safety dimension. Can this be repaired, how old is this tyre, is this wear pattern dangerous, can I drive on it for a week. Every one of those has a correct answer that depends on physical inspection, and none has an answer that survives being written generically.

That shapes how these tools should be used. They are excellent at the administrative half of the business — quote replies, reminders, follow-ups, explanations of what is included — and unreliable on the assessment half. Keep that boundary clear and the time saving is real.

Quote replies that stop the price-shopping

The same tyre is cheap somewhere else, so competing on price alone is a losing position. The workable structure answers four things in order: fitted price including everything, how long it takes, what is included, and what warranty or roadside provision applies.

The third item carries the value. Quotes that name balancing, new valves, disposal and torque-setting separately look higher and close better than a single figure that the customer assumes is comparable with a competitor's bare number.

Where they failed

Puncture repair. Generated text writes a confident yes. In practice whether a puncture can be repaired depends on where it is, its size, how long the tyre has been run deflated, and whether the structure is damaged. Write the policy as a set of categories with an inspection step, naming which cases you cannot repair and why, and be explicit that the assessment happens on the tyre, not in the message.

Tyre age. Customers increasingly ask how old the tyres are, correctly. State your policy plainly: you supply new stock, date codes are shown before fitting, and anything older than your stated threshold is not fitted. Do not let generated prose give a definitive shelf life — guidance on serviceable age varies between manufacturers and authorities, so name whose guidance you follow.

Driveability. "You can drive on that for a while" is not a sentence a shop should publish. Whether a tyre is safe to drive on depends entirely on inspection. Say what requires immediate attention and that this cannot be assessed from a description or photograph.

TPMS and modern vehicles. Sensor relearning and reset procedures differ enough between vehicles that generated instructions here are actively unhelpful. State that you handle it, note which vehicles need extra time, and stop there.

What customers read first

Total fitted price, wait time, and whether they need an appointment. Lead with all three. The rest of the explanation belongs below them, and in our experience the single line that most reduces inbound calls is stating whether the quoted online price already includes fitting and balancing.

Two worth adding

A seasonal storage note, if you offer it — what you label, where it is kept, and what happens if collection is late. And a torque re-check offer: asking customers to return for a re-torque after a short distance is a real safety practice, and writing it down reads as competence.

Written warranties customers can actually use

A tyre warranty written in generalities helps nobody. State what is covered, for how long, and what the customer has to do to claim — normally returning the vehicle to you and presenting the original invoice. Say whether coverage follows the tyre or the original purchaser, because customers assume transfer more often than not. Manufacturer warranties and your own workshop guarantee are different documents; naming which is which in the quote removes a recurring source of confusion at the counter.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Quote replies, reminders and review requests: 2–4 minutes. Anything touching repair eligibility, tyre age or whether something is safe to drive: 6–10 minutes, and it is all removal of false confidence. Each one of those answers depends on looking at the tyre, and no draft can do that.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI writing tools handle my quote replies?

Yes, well. The structure is the same every time, which is exactly the case these tools suit. Supply sizes, brands, fitted price and what is included, and check those fields rather than the prose.

How should I write about puncture repairs?

As a policy with categories and an inspection step. Repair eligibility depends on location, size, prior run-flat damage and structural condition — none of which can be assessed from a message, so say that plainly.

What should I tell customers about tyre age?

State your own supply policy and show date codes before fitting. Avoid asserting a universal serviceable life; manufacturer and regulatory guidance differ, so name whose guidance you follow and encourage the customer to check their own vehicle documents.

Can I say whether a tyre is safe to drive on?

Not from a description or photograph, and no generated text should either. State that safe/unsafe is determined by physical inspection and specify what conditions require the vehicle not to be driven.

How do I compete with online tyre prices?

By itemising what the fitted price includes — balancing, valves, disposal, torque setting — and stating total wait time. Customers comparing against bare online figures often find the fitted total closer than it first looked.
