Tutoring writing has two audiences with opposite needs. The parent wants reassurance and a summary they can act on. The record — your session notes — wants precision, because in three months you will be asked what you covered in February. Generated text is very good at producing confident summaries, which is exactly the risk: a confident summary of a session where nothing was mastered.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Session recaps for parents. Same five headings every time: covered, went well, needs work, practising this week, next step. Written dozens of times a week with near-identical structure.
  • Practice feedback. Returning marked work with a consistent note shape — what improved, one thing to fix, one thing to try.
  • Lesson plan outlines. Objective, sequence, checks for understanding, homework. Faster from a template than from memory each Sunday.
  • Topic explainers. Rewriting a concept at three reading levels, which is genuinely useful and genuinely tedious.
  • Invoices and reminders. What was covered, what is owed, the cancellation policy stated plainly.
  • Profile and marketing copy. What you teach, who you teach, how sessions run — written once, reused everywhere.
  • Cancellation and scheduling messages. Short, repeated, and better worded kindly than written in a hurry.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Invented progress. "Good grasp of fractions now" generated from a session where the student got three out of ten. This is the failure that matters: a parent makes decisions based on that sentence.
  • Assessment claims. Predicted grades, exam readiness, percentile statements. A tutor's written estimate carries weight and creates expectations you cannot control.
  • SEND and accommodation wording. Anything describing a student's needs, diagnoses or adjustments. Write these yourself, from what parents and schools have told you, and keep them confidential.
  • Homework instructions. Generated instructions that are vaguer than the ones in the exercise book, which produces emails at 9pm.
  • Comparing students. Any phrasing that ranks one child against another, including implicit comparisons in an update.
  • Confidentiality. Names and details pasted into a tool you have not checked the retention policy on. Worth knowing before, not after.

What to write down while it is fresh

A recap written two days later describes the session you remember, not the one that happened. Two habits fixed most of ours. First, note what was attempted, not what was understood — "[name] got 7/12 on expanding brackets, errors were all sign errors" survives translation into a parent update; "struggled with algebra" does not. Second, write tomorrow's starting point immediately, because it is obvious at 5pm and gone by Thursday. Those two lines are all a template needs to produce something accurate. Anything else — predicted progress, comparisons to expectations, adjectival encouragement — belongs nowhere near a parent update, and it is the part generated drafts add unprompted.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a tutor the saving concentrates in the recap and the practice feedback: two short documents written thirty times a week, where a template genuinely removes work.

A workflow that holds up

Keep your notes raw and specific: which questions were attempted, which were correct, what the error pattern was. Give the tool those notes and ask for shape, not substance. Write the judgement sentence yourself — the "she is close, but X" line is the one parents read and the only one that has to be exactly right. Never generate anything about a student's needs or diagnoses. Check the tool's data retention terms before pasting any real name, and use a first name or initials regardless. Then reread each recap as a parent reads it: is there one thing they can do tonight, and does it say what you actually observed?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write session recaps for parents?

Yes for the recurring structure — covered, went well, needs work, next step. Write the judgement sentence yourself; a confident summary of a session where nothing was mastered misleads parents.

Is it safe to include student names?

Check the tool data retention and training terms first, and prefer initials regardless. Anything about a student needs, diagnoses or adjustments should not go into a generator at all.

Can it write predicted grades or exam readiness?

No. A written estimate from a tutor carries real weight with parents and creates expectations you cannot control.

What should a tutor set up first?

The five-heading session recap and the practice-feedback note. Both repeat constantly and both benefit more from consistency than from clever phrasing.
