Financial writing is the one category where the risk is not embarrassment, it is a regulator reading a sentence you signed. A model will write a confident paragraph about returns, risk tolerance or a product's features in about four seconds, and every one of those sentences is your problem once it goes to a client. Used inside a review process, the tools save real time. Used as a shortcut, they create documents that look fine and read as advice you did not intend.

Where AI clearly helps

  • First drafts for review. Internal drafting, where a sentence gets rewritten or cut before it ever reaches a client. The tool is fastest here, because a bad draft costs nothing.
  • Meeting follow-ups and scheduling. The confirmation, the recap of what was discussed, what happens next. Short, factual, low risk.
  • Service and process pages. What a first meeting looks like, what documents are needed, how reviews run. Process writing, not advice writing.
  • Seminar and workshop invites. Date, place, format, what to bring, who should not attend if a condition applies.
  • Internal checklists and onboarding. Compliance file order, what goes where, what to verify before sending.
  • Plain-language glossaries. Defining a term in your own words once and reusing it consistently.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Performance language. Any generated sentence about returns, projections or comparisons that was never marked for review. This is the fastest way to create a document you cannot stand behind.
  • Product feature descriptions. Fees, terms, exit conditions or what a product is designed for. Those come from the product document, not from a model.
  • Risk-profile wording. Saying someone is conservative or aggressive when nothing in the file says so.
  • Tax and estate statements. Anything touching tax treatment, thresholds or succession rules. Those change and must be checked against the current rules.
  • Client confidentiality. A generated recap containing a real portfolio's actual figures, pasted into another system before anyone noticed.
  • Assumptions stated as facts. "You will need to retire at 62" written because it rhymed, when the client's file says something else.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For an advisor the honest framing is different: the model shortens the blank page, and the review step is unchanged. If your review process already takes an hour per client letter, that hour is not going away.

A workflow that holds up

Split every document into two halves: the process half and the advice half. The process half — scheduling, checklists, invites, service pages — can be generated freely and reviewed quickly. The advice half — performance, projections, product wording, anything about a client's situation — is written from source documents and reviewed by whoever owns compliance, with the model used only to tidy the sentences afterwards. Keep a confidential-facts file per client so numbers are never typed into a prompt. Before sending, read the letter the way a client will: the first sentence should say what happened, and the last should say what they need to do. If a paragraph promises a future outcome, cut it, even if it reads well.
