Real Estate Listing Description Templates: 9 Structures and the Phrases to Avoid

A listing description has two jobs: get the showing, and not create a problem afterwards. Generated copy is good at the first and unreliable at the second. These nine templates handle the structure; the section on risky phrases handles the rest.

Listing copy is the rare format where a generated sentence can cost more than it earns. Most marketing text is exaggeration with no consequence. A description that implies a school district, a neighborhood character, or a suitability for a type of person can create a real problem — and models produce exactly those implications without being asked, because they are common in the training data.

How to use these templates

Fill every [bracket] from the property file: measurements, year, materials, HOA, taxes. Never let a number appear in the draft that you have not verified, because a generated square footage or lot size is the most common and most expensive error in this category. Keep the first sentence about the property, not about the market. And check the finished text against your local fair-housing rules before it goes live — the template gives you structure, not compliance.

1. The single-family home

  • Opening: the one thing that will make someone book — [N]-bed, [N]-bath on [lot size] with [specific feature].
  • The main floor: layout in the order a visitor walks it.
  • Kitchen and living: appliances, surfaces, light, year of any renovation.
  • Bedrooms and baths: count, sizes, which has the [feature].
  • Outside: lot, orientation, outbuildings, parking.
  • Systems: roof year, HVAC year, water heater — the section buyers scan for.
  • Location facts: distance to [named place], transit, taxes, HOA. Facts only.
  • Close: showing instructions.

2. The condo or apartment

  • Opening: floor, exposure, and the view or light.
  • Interior: layout, finishes, in-unit laundry, storage.
  • Building: amenities, elevator, parking, security, age of building.
  • The monthly number: HOA or strata fee and what it covers. State it plainly — hiding it produces wasted showings.
  • Location facts: transit, walkable destinations with real names.
  • Close: what is included and showing instructions.

3. The rental listing

  • Opening: price, bedrooms, availability date.
  • The space: rooms, light, storage, laundry.
  • Terms: lease length, deposit, utilities included, pets policy, parking.
  • Building and area: facts, not characterisations.
  • Application: what a tenant needs to provide.

4. The new build

  • Opening: completion status and price.
  • Specification: finishes, systems, warranty — named, not described.
  • Floor plans: what is standard and what is an upgrade, clearly separated.
  • Timeline: completion date and what remains.
  • Close: model home location or appointment process.

5. The fixer-upper

  • Opening: be direct about condition — [N]-bed needing [specific work], priced for it.
  • What is sound: roof, foundation, systems, with years.
  • What needs work: named, with rough scope. Honesty here filters the wrong showings.
  • Potential: what the space allows, described as possibility not as fact.
  • Close: financing notes if relevant, sold-as-is statement if applicable.

6. The luxury listing

  • Opening: one specific detail instead of an adjective — the material, the maker, the dimension.
  • Provenance or design: architect, year, and what was original.
  • Rooms: described by use and light, not by superlatives.
  • Grounds and outbuildings: specifics.
  • Close: private showing process.

Adjectives are the failure mode here. Generated luxury copy reaches for "stunning", "breathtaking" and "unparalleled" within the first paragraph, and at that price the reader is looking for facts.

7. The commercial listing

  • Opening: square footage, zoning, price per square foot.
  • Specification: ceiling height, loading, power, parking ratio, HVAC.
  • Traffic or access facts: counts from a source, road names, transit.
  • Tenancy: current tenants, lease terms, escalations if disclosable.
  • Close: who to contact for the package.

8. Land and lots

  • Opening: acreage, zoning, and what is permitted.
  • Utilities: what is at the lot line and what is not. The deciding factor for land.
  • Access: road frontage, easements, legal access.
  • Physical: topography, tree cover, water, flood designation if known.
  • Close: survey availability.

9. The neighbourhood-facing page

For agents building area pages rather than listings. Describe amenities as facts with names and distances — [park name] is [distance] away — and avoid any characterisation of who lives there. This is the page most likely to drift into risky language when generated.

The phrases to remove before publishing

Our testing surfaced a consistent set of generated constructions worth deleting on sight. Any description of the people in an area — who lives there, who it suits, what kind of buyer would like it. This is the highest-risk pattern and models reach for it unprompted. Proximity claims stated loosely: "minutes from" without a number, or a distance nobody measured. School references unless the assignment is verified and permitted in your market. Unverified numbers: square footage, lot size, ceiling height, age — all of which a model will supply with total confidence and no source. Adjective stacks: three modifiers in a row is the signature of generated copy and readers have learned to see it. Promises about the future: planned developments, rezoning, value expectations.

What this costs, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For an agent listing several properties a month, the saving is real but smaller than it looks, because the verification step cannot be shortened. The draft is fast; checking it against the file is not.

A workflow that holds up

Keep a property fact sheet — measurements, years, fees, taxes, utilities — and paste it into the prompt as the source. Ask for structure first and adjectives second, or do not ask for adjectives at all. Every number in the output gets checked against the fact sheet, not against memory. Then run one specific pass for the risky patterns: people, schools, loose distances, future promises. Anything that describes who the home is suited to, delete rather than soften. If your brokerage has an approved phrasing list, paste it in as a standing instruction so the same corrections stop recurring.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write a full listing description?

It can produce the structure and the phrasing reliably. Every number — size, age, fees, taxes, distances — has to come from your property file and be checked against it afterwards.

What generated phrasing creates the most risk?

Any description of the people in an area or of who a home suits. Models produce this unprompted because it is common in listing copy, and it is the pattern most likely to breach fair-housing rules.

Should I let AI write the neighbourhood section?

Area pages are the highest-risk format. Keep them to named amenities and measured distances, and let a person review anything that characterises a place or its residents.

Does generated copy hurt search visibility for listings?

The risk is duplication, not the fact that it was generated. Hundreds of listings with identical phrasing add nothing; the differentiating facts are what make a page worth indexing.
