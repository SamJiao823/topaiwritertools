Vehicle copy is unusual because almost every sentence is a fact that matters legally. Price, fees, finance terms, mileage, service history, warranty status. Retail copy elsewhere tolerates approximation; here a wrong number is a disclosure problem and often a regulatory one. That single constraint should decide how you use generation: the tool does the shape, you do every figure.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Vehicle listing structure. The recurring order — basics, condition, features, history, terms — applied consistently across a changing inventory.
  • Service reminder campaigns. Interval, mileage elapsed, what is due, booking link. Hundreds a month with identical structure.
  • Lead follow-up sequences. Day one, day three, day seven, each with a distinct purpose rather than three versions of "just checking in".
  • Finance and warranty explainers. Written once at a plain reading level, reused forever. This is where education actually helps conversion.
  • Trade-in enquiry replies. What you need to value it: registration, mileage, service history, photos.
  • Service department updates. Diagnosis found, work recommended, cost, timing, approval needed.
  • Review requests. One short message after handover, with the link, sent once.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Prices, fees and "on the road" figures. A generated drive-away price that omits a charge is the fastest route to an unhappy customer in the showroom.
  • Finance terms and APR. Representative examples, term lengths, deposit assumptions. Regulated in most markets, and a generated example that looks official is worse than none.
  • Condition descriptions. "Immaculate", "full service history", "one owner" applied to a car nobody has checked. These are representations about the vehicle.
  • Mileage, MOT or inspection dates. Always stated as a figure with the date you checked it — read from the record, never recalled by a model.
  • Availability promises. "Still available" or "reserve today" on a unit that sold last week, which is how you get an awkward Saturday phone call.
  • Optional extras and packs. Naming a package the specific trim does not include, because such packages appear constantly in used-car prose.

The handover message nobody writes well

The day a customer collects a car is the easiest moment to earn a review and the easiest to waste. A good handover message does three things: confirms what was agreed one last time, tells them who to call about what, and asks for one specific thing — usually a review, once, with the link. Generated versions tend to overreach: they add a service reminder, a referral ask and a "we value your business" closer, and the reader does none of them. Keep it to the three. Include the one person's name they should contact, because "contact the dealership" is what people write when nobody wants to be responsible.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a dealership the honest saving is in the service reminders and the follow-up sequences; listings save less than expected because every fact has to be verified against the stock record anyway.

A workflow that holds up

Pull listing data from your inventory system and paste that as the source — do not describe the car in prose and hope the draft stays accurate. Generate the condition section as a checklist rather than as narrative, so gaps are visible rather than papered over with adjectives. Keep finance and warranty wording in approved blocks that are never generated. Have the availability line set by whatever actually knows the stock, not by the draft. Then spot-check a handful of live listings against the physical vehicle before you publish fifty; the errors cluster in the same two or three fields, and once you know which, you will fix them in the template.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write vehicle listings?

It can produce consistent structure across a changing inventory. Every fact — price, fees, mileage, service history, extras — must come from your stock record and be checked against the physical vehicle.

Is it safe for finance or APR wording?

No. Use approved blocks for anything regulated, including representative examples and term assumptions. A generated official-looking finance example creates real exposure.

What is the highest-risk sentence in car copy?

A condition claim — "full service history", "one owner", "immaculate" — applied to a car nobody verified. Those are representations about the vehicle.

Where does it save the most time?

Service reminder campaigns and lead follow-up sequences. Both are high-volume, repetitive and low-risk once the facts are correct.
