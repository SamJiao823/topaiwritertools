The 5-step workflow that doubled our usable output

This is the workflow we used every day. It looks boring. It works.

  1. Define the goal and one reader. Write one sentence: who is this going to, and what do you want them to do? "Follow up with Sarah, who didn't reply, and get a yes or no by Friday." If you can't write that sentence, the AI can't either.
  2. Build the prompt. Four parts: role, context, task, constraints. Paste real facts — a name, a date, a link, an amount. More real context is the single biggest lever on quality.
  3. Generate 2–3 variations. Don't take the first draft. Ask for "a shorter version" and "a more direct version." The second one is usually the one.
  4. Edit for voice and specifics. Cut the filler, swap generic words for real ones, and make sure the call-to-action matches your actual goal.
  5. Pre-send check. Read it as the recipient. Did it open with something that matters to them? Is the ask obvious? Would you answer this email? If you wouldn't, fix it.

That last step is non-negotiable. AI drafts for you; you send for you.

How do I prompt AI to write a cold email?

Cold email is where most people fail, because they prompt like this: "write a cold email to a gym owner about my web design services." That returns a draft that mentions "synergy" and reads like it was sent to 5,000 strangers. Our template kills most of that in one pass:

ROLE tells the model who to sound like. CONTEXT gives it facts instead of assumptions. TASK is the exact deliverable. CONSTRAINTS are the guardrails that stop the AI-isms.

PartWhat it doesExample
RoleSets the voice and perspective"You are a freelance web designer who builds booking features for local businesses."
ContextGives the model real facts to work with"My prospect is Mike, owner of Ironworks Gym. His site has no online booking, and I noticed it on a Google search for 'gym near downtown'."
TaskNames the exact deliverable and length"Write a cold email under 120 words with one subject line, asking for a 15-minute call this week."
ConstraintsBlocks the AI clichés and controls tone"No flattery, no 'I hope this finds you well', no yes/no questions, one specific call-to-action."

Fill in the bracketed parts and you have a cold email prompt in 60 seconds.

5 copy-paste email prompts that actually work

Every prompt below is one we ran through Rytr and Jasper and edited in real outreach. Copy, paste, replace the bracketed bits.

Prompt 1 · Cold email You are a freelance web designer who adds online booking to local business websites. My prospect is [name], owner of [business]. His/her site at [url] has no booking feature, and I noticed it while searching for [service + city]. Goal: get a 15-minute call this week. Task: write a cold email under 120 words with one subject line. Constraints: no flattery, no "I hope this finds you well", no yes/no questions, mention one specific problem I saw on the site, end with a single call-to-action.
Prompt 2 · Follow-up (no reply) You are me, a [role] at [company]. I emailed [name] about [topic] last [day] and got no reply. Task: write a follow-up under 90 words with one subject line. Constraints: don't apologize, don't reference the last email, add exactly one new piece of value ([example: a case study of a similar company]), keep it to 3 sentences, end with a yes-or-no question that's easy to answer.
Prompt 3 · Sales reply (objection) Customer wrote: "Your tool looks good but it's out of our budget this quarter." Task: write a reply that acknowledges the budget, reframes the ROI in one concrete sentence, and keeps the door open for [quarter]. Constraints: warm tone, no discounting, under 100 words, one soft question at the end.
Prompt 4 · Product launch You are a product marketer at [company], a [product type]. We just launched [feature]. Task: write a 150-word launch email to existing users, with one subject line and one call-to-action button ("[CTA text]"). Constraints: excited but not shouty, max 3 exclamation points, include the word "finally" once, lead with the user benefit, not the feature.
Prompt 5 · Customer service Customer emailed: "I was charged twice for my subscription. Fix this." Here are our policies: [paste policy, e.g. refunds within 30 days]. Task: write a first response under 80 words that acknowledges the error, states exactly what I'll do, and gives a timeline. Constraints: apologize exactly once, do not blame the customer, no jargon, one next step.

When you should NOT use AI to write an email

We're not going to tell you AI is right for every email, because it isn't. After 30 days, here's the line we drew:

  • Sensitive or legally loaded topics. Layoffs, health, legal disputes, money owed, anything with regulatory language. A wrong tone here costs more than a draft saves.
  • Highly personalized messages. A reply to a long-term client, a mentor, or someone who just lost a family member. If you know the person, you write it.
  • Apologies after a real mistake. AI drafts read like you handed the apology to a middle manager. Own it in your own words.
  • Anything you can't verify. Never let AI state a fact, price, date, or promise you haven't checked yourself. That's how companies send emails that embarrass them.

Rule of thumb from our testing: if a wrong tone costs you a relationship or money, write it yourself. If a wrong tone costs you 10 minutes of rewording, let AI draft it and you edit.

Can AI write professional emails?

Yes, for most business email types. In our 30-day test, structured prompts produced about 79% send-ready drafts for cold emails, follow-ups, sales replies, launches, and support tickets. Sensitive or highly personalized emails still need a human.

Is it okay to use AI for cold emails?

It's legal if you follow spam rules and include honest sender details — but you should still edit drafts to add real specifics and your own voice. And if the recipient asks, don't hide that a message was automated.

How do I make AI emails sound less robotic?

How long does it take to write an email with AI?
