Two document types carried the value: quotes with specification blocks, and proof approval messages. A third — artwork requirements — worked well because the content is genuinely standard, and a fourth, installation and compliance terms, we split into administrative and site-specific parts.

The recurring problem with generated text in this trade is confident specification. Drafts stated resolutions, material grades, wind ratings and fixing methods as though standard, and those values depend on the job, the substrate and local rules.

Where it helped

Quotes with specification blocks

The structure worth reusing: dimensions, material, finish, print method, fixing method, quantity, lead time, and exclusions. Drafting went from about 20 minutes to 6, because the block transfers between jobs while the values change.

Proof approval messages

What to check in the proof — spelling, dimensions, colour reference, bleed and safe area, fixing positions — plus what approval means, and what happens after it. Generated drafts were good here, and adding an explicit statement that approval is final and production starts on receipt removed most later disputes.

Artwork requirements

Preferred formats, resolution expectations, colour mode, how to supply fonts, and what happens if the supplied file is not usable. This is standard content that drafting handles well. State the resolution expectation as a requirement, not a suggestion, and offer to check files before production.

Where it failed

Material and durability claims

Drafts described expected lifespan, weather resistance and suitability for specific conditions. How long a sign lasts depends on material, exposure, installation and maintenance, and stating an expectation in writing becomes a promise. Keep it to what the manufacturer documents.

Wind loading and structural specifics

Generated text named wind ratings, footing depths and fixing specifications. These are engineering matters determined by site conditions and local code, and for anything sizeable they need a qualified assessment.

Installation and access terms

Drafts assumed standard access and straightforward fixing. Whether an install is straightforward depends on the wall, the height, the substrate and what is behind it. Access is a mandatory field collected at quoting, not an assumption in the document.

Regulatory or permit statements

Text referred to signage rules as though known. Signage controls vary by council, zone and sometimes by street, and in many places require approval. Name the authority, or omit it.

What customers actually check

Someone ordering a sign or a print run wants to know: will it look like the mock-up, how long will it take, and is installation included. In our testing, quotes with an explicit "what happens if the artwork is not print-ready" line converted better and generated fewer complaints afterwards.

Also worth stating: what the proof covers and what it does not — a screen proof shows layout and colour reference, not the finished substrate appearance.

Artwork files: the biggest time sink

Unusable artwork consumes more shop time than any other single cause, and nearly all of it is preventable with one standard sheet sent at booking. State preferred formats, minimum resolution, colour mode, and how to supply fonts, then offer to check files before production. Where a supplied file cannot be used at size, say what the options are — redraw, re-supply, or accept the quality limit — before the job starts rather than after.

Proofs and what approval means

A proof approval is a decision with a cost attached, and treating it as casual produces disputes later. State what the proof shows and what it does not — layout and colour reference, not the finished appearance on the chosen substrate. State that approval is final and production begins on receipt. Where colour accuracy matters, name the reference system used and note that screens differ from printed output.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Quotes went from roughly 20 minutes to 6, near $7 saved at $30 per hour. Proof messages from about 11 minutes to 3. Artwork requirement sheets dropped to near zero drafting time after the first build, and are reused on every job.

A workflow that holds up

Template the quote with a specification block that has mandatory fields for material, dimensions and fixing. Send the proof with a fixed checklist and a clear statement that approval is final. Reuse one artwork requirements sheet. Wind loading, structural specifications, permit requirements and durability expectations come from assessment, code and manufacturer documentation — verified for the site, and written by whoever is accountable.

Worth adding to every job file: a photo of the installed result and a note on what the customer was told to expect. When a question comes up months later, that record settles it quickly, without anyone relying on memory.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write our sign quotes?

The structure, yes — with material, dimensions and fixing method filled from the site assessment.

Should it state expected lifespan?

Only what the manufacturer documents. A stated expectation becomes a promise.

Can it specify wind ratings or footings?

No. Those are engineering matters set by site conditions and local code.

What about signage permits?

Name the authority to check with, or leave it out. Rules vary by council and zone.

Should the proof note explain colour limits?

Yes — state that a screen proof shows layout and colour reference, not final substrate appearance.

Is this worth it for a small shop?

The artwork requirements sheet pays back immediately, since unusable files are the biggest time sink.
