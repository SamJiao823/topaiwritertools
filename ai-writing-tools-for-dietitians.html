The nutrition field has the highest writing risk of any profession on this list. Nearly every sentence can be read as a health claim. The useful workflow is not "should I use AI" — it is "which parts of my writing can a model touch".

Where AI clearly helps

  • Intake and session summaries. Recap what was discussed, what the client will do before the next visit. Neutral, factual, easy to generate.
  • Meal-plan explanations. Why this structure, what the portions mean, how to swap ingredients. The reasoning, written clearly.
  • Standard handouts. A grocery list template, a "what to log" page, a restaurant guide. Repetitive and boring, which is exactly what a model is good at.
  • Practice and profile pages. Services, formats, what a first appointment involves. Marketing-adjacent but factual.
  • Myth-busting explainers. The format is excellent for a model: state the claim, state the evidence, state the nuance.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Treatment claims. "Manages diabetes", "treats IBS", "resolves deficiencies" — in most jurisdictions these are the domain of a physician, and for a dietitian they are a licensing boundary. A model will write them without blinking.
  • Dosage and supplement numbers. It invents amounts, forms and upper limits. Supplement guidance regulated in your country is not a model output.
  • Individual advice on a real client file. This one stays human. No tool has the chart, the labs and the history.
  • Weight-loss promises. "Lose 10 pounds in two weeks" is a claim a regulator reads closely, whatever your intentions.
  • Contradictory handouts. One generated page says limit caffeine, another says it is fine in moderation. Clients bring the contradiction back to you.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer, versus roughly 8 minutes on the general assistant — around $7 per piece of time at $30/hour. For a dietitian billing $80 to $150 a session, the honest answer is that the hourly argument is weak. The real gain is the handout you rewrite every client and the newsletter you never send because there is no time.

A workflow that holds up

Write one master document per topic — portion sizes, the swap table, the grocery template — and generate the personalised version from it, then delete whatever does not apply to this client. Never send a generated medical statement as-is.

Practically: use AI for the administrative and educational half and keep the clinical sentences yours. If a sentence would need a signature to be defensible, it does not come from a model.

Also build a claim list. Every handout inherits your wording about supplement forms and condition language; version control that list once and the contradictions stop.

What dietitians ask

Can AI write client meal-plan explanations?

Yes, from a master template. The reasoning and structure are fine; any claim about a condition must be yours.

Will using AI risk my licence?

Only if a generated sentence crosses into diagnosis or treatment. Anything a physician could be held to has to be reviewed by you.
