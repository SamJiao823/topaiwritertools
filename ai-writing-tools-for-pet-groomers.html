The grooming business runs on repetitive text. Same bath-and-tidy description, same coat-care instructions, same reminder that a matted dog needs to come in sooner. AI is good at that half of the job. It is much worse at the half where a client is upset because their dog came out shaved.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Post-appointment care cards. Brush daily, no homework for 48 hours, avoid the ears — every groomer writes these every day. Generate one and edit the two lines that changed.
  • Service menu descriptions. Bath, tidy, full groom, de-shed, flea and tick treatment. Short, factual, identical. A model does it in a minute.
  • Breed cut explanations. A scissor finish versus a clipper cut, the reason a terrier undercoat needs hand stripping. True domain vocabulary makes the difference.
  • Follow-up reminders. The six-week check-in that keeps a client on the calendar. Polite, brief, no personality required.
  • Review replies. Calm, specific, no defensiveness. That is a template job.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Health claims about coats. "Hypoallergenic", "cures shedding", "therapeutic for skin conditions" — these are regulated statements. A model has no idea what a wheaten terrier's coat does in July. Never ship a claim about a dog's skin without a vet's input.
  • Advising on a medical presentation. If a dog is flaky, mats in one spot, or smells bad in a specific way, that is a vet conversation. An AI will happily write you a care plan.
  • Made-up coat types. It will invent a "low-maintenance mixed coat" that does not describe the animal in your chair.
  • Care cards that contradict each other. One generated card says avoid the ears, another says clean them daily. Clients read the card at home and then blame you.
  • Animalsexist phrasing. The classic one is writing about a nervous dog as if it were naughty; the client hears that and it becomes the next review.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the premium tool — roughly $7 per piece of your time at $30/hour. Groomers bill $50 to $120 a session, so the argument here is not hourly economics. It is the eight hours a month spent typing the same care card.

A workflow that holds up

Keep a breed facts file — coat type, recommended clip, typical matting spots, the two sentences you say out loud to every client. Generate the care card from that file, then change only the sentences that differ. For the service menu, generate once, then write the exact price and duration next to each entry. Prices and minutes are the part a model must never guess.

Finally, read any care card the way a tired client reads it at 9pm. If the instruction is not obvious then, rewrite it.
