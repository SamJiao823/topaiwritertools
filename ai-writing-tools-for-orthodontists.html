Appointment reminders

The structure that works: date, time, what to bring (aligners, elastics, insurance card), and the reschedule link. This is repeatable and exactly what these tools do cleanly. The only fact it cannot invent is the slot, so supply that.

Consent explainers

A good explainer describes what a procedure involves and the kinds of things a patient should know — in plain language, without diagnosing or promising an outcome. Generated drafts are a starting point, but the clinical content must be reviewed and approved by the treating clinician, because the specifics depend on the individual case.

Where they failed

Treatment outcomes and timelines. "Braces take 18 months" or "aligners fix this in 6 weeks" cannot be known without the individual assessment. Generated text will state a confident number. The correct framing is that duration and results vary by case and are set by the treating clinician after assessment — never a generic promise.

Clinical "advice." Any line telling a patient what is wrong, what to do for pain, or whether they need treatment is a diagnosis. Keep generated content to logistics and general education, and route any clinical question to the practice. Statements about what a patient "should" do clinically must come from the qualified provider.

Before/after claims. Showing representative results is fine with appropriate disclaimers; asserting a specific result for a new patient is not. Describe typical process and let the clinician's actual, approved cases speak.

FAQ that fills the phone line

What patients ask most: how long, does it hurt, what about food, what if something breaks, what does it cost. A clear FAQ answers these in plain language with the standing caveat that specifics are set at the individual consultation. The line that most reduces call volume is stating clearly that timelines and suitability are confirmed by the clinician, not guessed.

Two worth adding

An emergency guidance note — what counts as urgent versus manageable, and how to reach the practice — framed as logistics, not self-treatment. And a retainer/aftercare note describing the general importance of retention, with the individual plan set by the clinician.

The consult follow-up email

The message sent after a first consult is where practices quietly lose or keep a patient. The version that keeps them answers three things the clinician covered but the patient forgot: what was found, what the recommended path is, and the one next step with a date. Generated drafts can structure this well from the clinician's notes; the clinical content must be the clinician's, never invented by the tool, because the specifics depend on the individual case.

The line to keep out is any restated outcome or timeline — "your treatment will take about 18 months" should come from the consult notes, not a template. The follow-up should point the patient back to what was agreed with the clinician and name the next appointment, not re-diagnose or re-quote a result. In our testing the notes-based version reduced "can you remind me what we decided" calls to nearly zero, because the email already said it in plain language the patient could re-read. Patients who receive it within a day of the consult are the ones who book, because the detail is still fresh.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Reminders, FAQs and review requests: 2–4 minutes. Anything touching treatment description, outcomes or clinical guidance: 8–12 minutes, and it is all review by the treating clinician. Those sentences depend on the individual patient and professional judgement, and no draft can stand in for either.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI writing tools handle my practice reminders?

Yes, easily — appointment logistics, what to bring, and reschedule links are repeatable admin the tools do cleanly. The only fact to supply is the actual slot.

How should I write about treatment time?

As varying by case and set by the treating clinician after assessment, never as a confident generic number. Duration and outcomes depend on the individual.

Can generated text give clinical advice?

No. Any line diagnosing, prescribing, or telling a patient what to do clinically must come from the qualified provider. Keep generated content to logistics and general education.

What reduces phone calls most?

A clear FAQ stating that timelines and suitability are confirmed at the individual consultation, with the common questions (pain, food, cost, emergencies) answered in plain language.

Should I show before/after results?
