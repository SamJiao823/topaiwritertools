Three document types carried almost all the value: ticket responses, handover notes, and knowledge base articles. A fourth — client-facing incident explanation — turned out to be where the risk concentrated.

Where it clearly helped

Knowledge base articles

The largest gain, and the most durable. Turning a resolved ticket into a documented article is work everyone intends to do and nobody does. Generating a first draft from the ticket notes made it realistic: what the symptom was, what was checked, what resolved it, and how to confirm. Each article written once saves the same explanation repeatedly.

Handover notes between shifts

Where work passes between technicians, structured notes reduced repeated diagnosis. The fields that mattered: what was attempted, what the result was, what remains, and what to try next. Generated drafts handled the formatting; the content had to come from the technician.

Recurring ticket responses

Password resets, printer queues, access requests — responses that are the same every time, with one or two variables. Drafting them was quick and safe. The variable parts must be checked each time, since inserting the wrong account name is worse than a slower reply.

Where it nearly caused problems

Incident explanations to clients

Generated explanations stated causes with confidence the evidence did not support. During an incident, the cause is usually not yet established, and a confident statement that later proves wrong costs more than "cause under investigation". Describe what was observed and what was done; leave causation until it is known.

Steps that were not tested

Drafts suggested troubleshooting and remediation commands drawn from plausible patterns. On a client system, an untested command is a risk regardless of how reasonable it reads. Anything touching configuration, permissions or data should come from your own documented procedures.

Security statements

Generated text asserted that systems were unaffected or data was secure. Those are claims about someone else's environment and cannot be made from a language model — they require your own verification and your client's incident process.

What clients read in a monthly report

A monthly report is the only regular evidence a client has that the contract is worth its cost, and most are written as ticket counts. Two changes made them worth reading. Group by pattern rather than by ticket — "four password resets, three from the same team, all within a day of the policy change" tells a client something; "seven tickets closed" does not. Say what was fixed so it stops recurring, because that is what separates maintenance from firefighting, and it is the part clients forward to their own management.

Also worth building: the recurring-explanation library. A small number of requests generate most tickets, and a documented answer with screenshots that staff can find themselves is the cheapest reduction in volume available. Keeping it current takes a few minutes a week and it compounds.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

A knowledge base article that took about 40 minutes to write came down to roughly 14 minutes, near $13 saved at $30 per hour. Handover notes went from around 9 minutes to 3. Ticket responses improved least in relative terms — the drafts were fine but the variable fields needed checking every time.

A workflow that holds up

Convert resolved tickets into knowledge base articles on a schedule rather than when you remember. Use a fixed handover structure with mandatory fields for what was tried. Template the recurring responses and check the variables before sending. Incident causation, remediation steps and security statements come from your process — verified, documented, and written by whoever is accountable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write ticket responses?

For recurring requests, yes. Template them and check the variable fields — wrong account names or steps cause more work than the time saved.

Should it suggest remediation commands?

No. Untested commands on a client system are a risk. Use your documented procedures.

Can it write incident reports?

It can format them. State what was observed and done; establish causation through investigation, not drafting.

What about telling a client their data is safe?

That requires your own verification. Never generated.

Is the knowledge base the best use?

In our testing, yes — roughly 26 minutes saved per article, and the article keeps saving time afterwards.

Does it work with our ticketing system?

These tools produce text you paste in. Check integration support before expecting automation.
