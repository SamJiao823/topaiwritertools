Two document types carried most of the value: quote follow-ups and post-service explanations. A third — warranty and liability wording — we stopped generating entirely, and a fourth, maintenance reminders, worked well once the specifics were constrained.

The pattern matches other trades we have tested. Documents that repeat with different variables are worth generating; documents that carry safety or legal weight are not. Garage doors sit at the awkward end of that spectrum, because they are heavy, spring-loaded, and the failures are mechanical.

Where it helped

Quote follow-ups

A customer who asked for a price on a door replacement or an opener repair and has not booked needs one nudge, not a brochure. Generated versions were good once we stripped out the service description and left three things: the price, the slot still available, and one action. Drafting went from about 12 minutes to 3.

Post-service explanations

What was found, what was replaced, why it failed, and what to watch for. This is where generated drafts genuinely helped — the structure transfers between jobs and the model is good at ordering the explanation so it makes sense to a non-technical reader. Every factual field still gets filled from the job record, and in our testing the errors clustered in the "why it failed" line, where the model filled the gap with a plausible cause nobody had verified.

Maintenance reminders

The annual service nudge — lubricate, check balance, test the auto-reverse, inspect cables and springs. Generated reminders worked well because the content is genuinely standard. Keep it to what the customer can safely check themselves (visual inspection, the auto-reverse test) and route anything involving springs, cables or adjustment to a technician.

Where it failed

Spring and cable work

This is the line we do not cross. Torsion springs and lift cables are under high tension, and adjustment is a task for a trained technician with the right tools. Generated text described inspection and adjustment steps with the same confidence it used for everything else, which is exactly the failure mode that gets people hurt. Our rule: any content involving springs, cables, brackets under load, or opener force settings is written by a technician or not published at all.

Warranty and liability wording

Drafts produced warranty statements — coverage periods, what voids coverage, who bears the cost of a part failure — that were plausible and unauthorised. Warranty terms are a commitment your business has to honour, and they vary by manufacturer, part and installation. Keep them in a maintained document owned by a named person, and link to it rather than restating it.

Diagnostics from a description

Given a symptom, generated text offered a cause. Sometimes right, often enough to mislead. A diagnosis comes from looking at the door, not from a description of the noise. We now write these as "what we will check when we arrive" rather than "what is wrong".

Specific part or model claims

Drafts named opener models, spring sizes and compatibility statements. Part numbers, spring wire sizes and cycle ratings have to come from the door itself and the manufacturer's documentation — guessing them produces a quote that cannot be honoured.

What customers actually read

Someone with a door that will not open reads three things: when can you come, how much, and is it safe to leave it like this. Answering the third question honestly — including "leave it closed and do not force it" — does more for trust than any reassurance about response times.

Worth adding to every quote: what is covered by the parts warranty, in writing, on the quote. In our testing this was the single most common follow-up question, and answering it up front removed most of the back-and-forth.

What to say when it is an emergency

A door that will not open is not a scheduled job, and the message for it should be different: current response window, whether the call-out is chargeable, and what to do meanwhile. Keep the safety line first and fixed — do not attempt to lift a door with a broken spring, and keep people away from the opening. The rest of the message matters less than being honest about when you will arrive.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Quote follow-ups went from about 12 minutes to 3, near $5 saved at $30 per hour. Post-service explanations fell from roughly 16 minutes to 6, with the diagnosis section written by hand. Maintenance reminders dropped from about 10 minutes to 2.

A workflow that holds up

Template the quote follow-up with mandatory fields for door type, opener model and warranty. Generate the post-service explanation from a fixed structure, then write the diagnosis yourself from the job record. Send maintenance reminders on an annual cycle. Spring, cable and force-setting content, plus all warranty terms, stay with the technician and your maintained documents — verified on site, owned by a named person, and written by whoever is accountable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write our garage door quotes?

The letter, yes — with door type, opener model and warranty fields filled in from the site visit.

Should it describe spring or cable work?

No. Tensioned hardware is technician-only content, both for the work and for anything describing it.

Can it diagnose from a description?

No. Write "what we will check" instead of stating a cause before seeing the door.

Should it name specific parts or sizes?

Only from the manufacturer's documentation and the measurements taken on site.

Do maintenance reminders work?

Yes, and they are safe to generate — provided they only cover what a customer can visually check.

Is this worth it for a small operation?

The quote follow-up pays back first, since most lost quotes are lost to silence rather than price.
