A welcome email is the only message a new subscriber is guaranteed to open. Open rates for the first welcome routinely clear 50%, against single digits for everything that follows, so the job is to do three things in that window: confirm what they got, tell them what happens next, and give one reason to act this week.

The mistake is bundling all three into one long message and hoping. People read the first screen and decide. Splitting the sequence across two or three short emails does more than any single clever subject line.

The three messages worth sending

1. Instant thank-youImmediatelyConfirm the actionWhat they signed up for, where to find it, one next step

Three is enough. A fifth or sixth welcome email is just a drip campaign wearing a friendly face, and it gets treated like one.

1. Instant thank-you (transactional)

Use when: someone just subscribed, bought, or created an account.

Subject: You're in — here's what to do first

Thanks for signing up for [program]. Here is exactly what you just got:

[What they purchased / subscribed to]
It is ready at [link].
The one thing worth doing now: [first action]. It takes about [time].

Reply to this email if anything is unclear — a real person reads this inbox.

Why this works: naming the single first action converts better than listing features, because it removes the "now what" gap that makes new users leave.

2. Setup nudge

Use when: 1–2 days after sign-up and they have not taken the first action yet.

Subject: The 10-minute step most people skip

You signed up for [program] but have not [first action] yet. That step is where most of the value shows up, so here is the shortest path:

1. Open [link]
2. Do [specific action]
3. You should see [expected result]

If you hit a wall, [support link] gets you unstuck.

Why this works: "most people skip" names the behaviour without blame, and gives a concrete result to look for.

3. Reason to return

Use when: 4–7 days after sign-up, once they have had value once.

Subject: One useful thing for [their role / interest]

Now that you have [first result], here is the next thing worth your time: [one resource]. No ask attached — just the part of [program] people tell us they wish they had found sooner.

Why this works: sending value with no ask rebuilds the open rate for everything after the welcome window.

What to leave out

No discount in email 1. A thank-you that opens with an upsell reads as the opposite of thanks. No seven-step onboarding. One action per email. No "we are a family" filler. New contacts want the thing they bought, not a mission statement.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Adapting a template to a new list: 3–6 minutes. Writing the setup nudge from scratch when the product has several first actions: 10–15 minutes, most of it deciding which single action to lead with. Get that choice wrong and the whole sequence underperforms.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many welcome emails should I send?

Three is the practical maximum for most lists: an instant thank-you, a setup nudge one or two days later, and a reason to return within the week. Beyond that it stops being a welcome and becomes a campaign.

Should the first welcome email include a discount?

No. Opening a thank-you with an upsell undercuts the moment. Save any offer for a later message once the contact has received what they signed up for.

What is the one thing every welcome email needs?

A single, named next action — the one step that delivers the promise. Listing features instead of one action is the most common reason new users drift.

Can AI write my welcome sequence?

Well, for structure and drafts. The tool cannot know which first action matters most for your product, so supply that and check it; the prose is then fast to produce.

When should the setup nudge go out?

One to two days after sign-up, aimed at the people who have not yet taken the first action. Earlier and it collides with the thank-you; later and the moment has passed.
