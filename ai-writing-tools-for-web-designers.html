Web design writing has a credibility problem that the tools make worse. Proposals read like the same proposal. Case studies quote numbers nobody checked. Page copy is polished and says nothing. The models are very good at producing that kind of text quickly, which means the differentiator moves further away from wording and further towards evidence.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Proposal structure. Approach, phases, what you need from the client, assumptions, the exclusions nobody reads. A model assembles this fast and consistently.
  • Scoping and discovery notes. Turning a messy hour of calls into numbered requirements with owners attached.
  • Page copy variants. The same hero line in three registers, for a client who cannot decide. Fast and easy to compare side by side.
  • Maintenance and status reports. Monthly, repetitive, and the document that quietly justifies the retainer.
  • Handoff documentation. Where things live, who to ask, backup routines, the IT contact's name.
  • Short follow-ups. The check-in before an invoice, the nudge for feedback, all of which nobody enjoys writing.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Case-study numbers. "Improved load time by 60%" or "lifted conversions" written because it sounded like a result. If it is not in the analytics, it does not go in the case study.
  • Performance guarantees. "Google 100 score" or "will rank first" as a promise. Performance work is measurable; the promise is not, and clients hold onto the sentence.
  • Credit and ownership. Case studies that blur whose work it was when you were the designer on someone else's project.
  • Third-party claims. Writing about a plugin, host or framework as if you tested it, or claiming a certification you did not complete.
  • Client details in training material. A real brand, real metrics and a real screenshot in a generated case study, published before the client agreed.
  • Security and uptime language. Uptime percentages, backup frequency, what happens in an outage. Those are operational promises from your actual host.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — close to $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. The saving that counts for a designer is the proposal: the same document rewritten for every lead, and usually written at midnight because nobody else will do it.

A workflow that holds up

Keep a reusable proposal skeleton and a per-client facts file with dates, fees, deliverables and any metric the client approved you to quote. Generate the proposal from the skeleton and the facts, then write the one paragraph that only you could write — the reason this design works for this business. For case studies, generate the structure and then insert the real numbers yourself, with the client's permission noted somewhere in your file. Page copy variants should be generated in one sitting and reviewed side by side, because the point is the comparison, not the words. Read the finished proposal out loud as the client will read it in an open office. If the second paragraph is a sentence about your studio, cut it.
