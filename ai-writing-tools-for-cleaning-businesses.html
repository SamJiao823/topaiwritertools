Cleaning is a service business that communicates in templates. The same three questions come up weekly: why does this cost what it costs, what happens if the job takes longer, and what should we not use on your stone counters. AI handles the first two. The third is where the liability sits.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Quote explanations. Square footage, number of bathrooms, inside appliances, whether the balcony counts. Turning a price into a defensible list of what is included stops most "that seems expensive" calls.
  • Move-in and move-out checklists. Long, boring, identical every time. Perfect model material.
  • Team SOPs. Supply list, order of operations, what to do with a locked door. One page, rewritten every job anyway.
  • Job follow-ups. The Tuesday-after check-in that gets you the next booking and the review.
  • Hiring posts. Pay range, schedule, what the job actually involves, what it does not.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Disinfection and sanitising claims. In the US those words are regulated — a product claim matters, and writing "we sanitise" on a marketing page without knowing which product did it is a problem.
  • Guaranteeing a result. "We guarantee a spotless home" becomes a refund conversation. Write what you will do, not what the result must measure up to.
  • Surface advice that is wrong. It will tell you vinegar is fine on marble with total confidence. Natural stone is not the only surface that would rewrite that sentence.
  • Chemical mixing instructions. If a client asks what to use and the answer involves two products, that is your job, not the model's.
  • Pet and child safety notes. Residue, drying time, where to keep the bottle. Get these from the product label, always.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Across our 30-day test the low-cost dedicated writer needed about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words versus roughly 8 minutes on the general assistant — around $7 per piece at $30/hour. For a one-person operation the cheaper plan is enough. For a crew of four, where a new hire reads the SOP on day one, the expensive tool is the one whose output actually survives contact with a real room.

A workflow that holds up

Write a rate card first: what you charge per square foot, per bathroom, and what pushes a job over. Generate the quote explanation from it, then paste the real numbers in. Keep one SOP document and version it — when the team grows, the document is the product.

For the client-facing side, generate three versions of the same checklist (move-in, move-out, deep clean) and keep the differences obvious. Clients forgive a checklist. They do not forgive a quote they cannot understand.
