Trade writing is mostly short, sent from a phone, and read by someone who is stressed. A burst pipe at 7pm is not a moment for carefully worded marketing. It is a moment for one clear sentence about when you will arrive. That constraint shapes everything: the useful text is short, factual and says the one thing the customer is waiting to hear.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Service page descriptions. What each job covers, what is typically included, what counts as an emergency. Written once, used everywhere.
  • Quote explanations. Why the price moved — parts, access, after-hours, how long the job took. The paragraph that prevents a dispute at the end of a job.
  • Emergency and callback messages. Short, calm, and consistent. Composing these between jobs is exactly when they get written badly.
  • Maintenance reminders. Annual boiler or water-heater service, drain checks, the seasonal nudge that fills a quiet month.
  • Review requests. A brief follow-up after a job, sent once, with no incentive that breaks platform rules.
  • Job handover notes. What was done, what to watch for, what needs a return visit.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Arrival time promises. "We will be there within the hour" generated into an auto-reply when you are two jobs away. A specific promise not kept is worse than a vague one kept.
  • Fixed prices for variable work. A model will happily state a price for a job nobody has seen. Quotes should describe the basis, not invent the number.
  • Licence and insurance statements. "Fully licensed and insured" without naming what or how much. Some customers will ask to see it, and a vague claim is worse than a specific one.
  • Guarantee wording. How long a repair is warranted, what voids it. That is a term you set, not a phrase to generate.
  • Diagnosis in text. A generated sentence explaining what is wrong with a system nobody has inspected. Describing a symptom is fine; diagnosing from a description is not.
  • Access and safety instructions. What the customer should do before you arrive — turning off a stopcock, isolating a supply. Those come from your own protocol.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a one-van operation the value is not the hourly math. It is the quote explanation and the callback message, both of which get written in a hurry and both of which decide whether the customer argues later.

A workflow that holds up

Write one service list and one quote-explanation template properly, then generate everything from them. Keep every number — price, arrival window, warranty period — out of the generated text and fill it in yourself, because those are the three things a customer will quote back at you. For emergency messages, keep one template with a deliberately honest window and never let a tool tighten it. When explaining a price change, generate the reasons first and then say the number in your own words. Read the finished message on a phone, standing up, the way the customer will.
