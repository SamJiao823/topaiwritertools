How to read the editing-time column

This is the number that decides whether a subscription survives its second month, and it is the one no pricing page shows. Generation is nearly free in time; editing is not. In our test, a thousand words from the low-cost dedicated writer needed around 22 minutes of correction before it was shippable, against around 8 for the general assistant. At $30 an hour that gap is roughly $7 of your time per piece — which is more than the monthly price difference between the two tools if you write more than a couple of pieces a month. The cheap tool is cheap in cash and expensive in minutes.

Where each type of tool fits

A dedicated writer pays off when your output is short, repetitive and high-volume — dozens of product blurbs, ad variants, social captions, simple emails. Templates save real time and the editing burden stays low because each piece is small. A general assistant pays off when your output is long, varied, or needs to hold an argument together across a few thousand words. You give up templates and get coherence. A search-facing tool earns its place only if publishing findable pages is a real part of your work; otherwise you are paying for a workflow you will not use. A team tool with brand voice is priced for the collaboration layer — if nobody else is writing, you are buying seats for one person.

The mistake the table is meant to prevent

People buy three of these and use one. The overlap is large: every tool here writes a paragraph, and the differences show up at the edges — length, workflow, voice enforcement. Before adding a second subscription, write down what the first one cannot do for you. If the answer is "nothing, it is just slow on long pieces", you need a general assistant, not another dedicated writer. If the answer is "it loses the thread past 800 words", same conclusion. Most stacks should be two tools at most: one for short repetitive volume, one for long documents.

What we did not measure

Being explicit about this, because it is where comparison charts usually overclaim. We did not run timed benchmarks on every tool in the table, so only two editing-time numbers are reported. We did not test team features with more than one person. We did not measure output quality with any automated score — AI detection scores and readability scores both move in ways that do not track whether a human would ship the text. And we did not test enterprise plans, because the pricing is negotiated and the comparison would not generalise.

A workflow that holds up

Pick based on the shape of your output, not the feature list. If most of what you write is short and repeated, start on the cheap dedicated tool and run a month of real work through it — not a demo workload. If most of it is long, start with the general assistant and paste your style guide in as a standing instruction. Use free trials to test your actual Tuesday, not an idealised task. Then, after a month, count the minutes you spent editing rather than the money you spent subscribing; the minutes are the larger number and the one nobody tracks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which AI writing tool should I pick?

It depends on the shape of your output. Short and repetitive suits a dedicated low-cost writer; long and varied suits a general assistant; findable pages can justify a search-facing tool; teams needing one voice justify a team plan.

Why is editing time in the table?

Because it is the cost nobody prices. Generation is fast; correction is what you pay for in minutes. In our test the gap between two tools was about $7 of time per piece at $30 an hour.

Do I need more than one tool?

Usually not. The overlap is large, and most stacks should be two at most: one for short repetitive volume, one for long documents. Write down what your current tool cannot do before adding another.
