Where it clearly helped

Shift reports that read consistently across officers. Twelve officers produce twelve writing styles, and a client reading monthly reports notices the difference in quality even when coverage was identical. A fixed structure — arrivals, patrols completed, exceptions, visitor or contractor activity, anything outstanding — levelled that considerably and made patterns visible across shifts.

Post orders and site instructions. Written once per site, in plain language, covering duties, patrol routes and frequencies, contacts, escalation numbers and what constitutes an incident requiring a call. Regenerating these consistently when sites change protected against versions drifting apart between the office copy and the one on site.

Client summaries. A weekly or monthly digest: activity, incidents by type, notable observations, any recommendations. Clients rarely read individual shift logs, and a readable summary became the thing they judged the service on.

Where it nearly went wrong

Incident narratives. Not delegated, at all. What the officer observed, in sequence, with times — written by that officer, shortly after, in their own words. A draft will tidy the sequence, add connecting detail and smooth the language, and every one of those changes is a problem if the report is later examined.

Anything describing people. Identifying characteristics, statements attributed to individuals, behaviour described. These need to be exact and attributable to a specific observer; generated description introduces detail that did not come from anyone.

Anything about force, arrest, or refusal of entry. Where powers were used or refused, the wording becomes the record of whether they were used lawfully. That belongs to the officer and your policy documents, not to a draft.

Why routine and incident reports are different documents

A patrol log's value is consistency — the same information every night, so that absences stand out. An incident report's value is precision, and its weakness is any sentence added for readability. The same tool serves the first and damages the second, which is why they should be produced differently rather than treated as variations of one document type.

What clients are buying, in practice

Security contracts are sold on presence and renewed on paperwork. The operators who kept clients longest over thirty days were not the ones with the most visible patrols — they were the ones whose monthly reporting let a facilities manager show something to their own management. A report that lists activity, exceptions and follow-ups gives a client evidence that the contract is worth its cost, which is what renewal decisions turn on. It also transforms complaints: an incident logged contemporaneously with times and actions is a very different document from one reconstructed when a client is already unhappy.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For security operations the saving lands in post orders, shift logs and client summaries; incident reports are excluded from drafting entirely, because reviewing generated narrative costs more than writing it.

A workflow that holds up

Standardise post orders per site and keep the on-site copy identical to the office copy. Use a fixed shift-log structure so exceptions are visible across officers. Write incident narratives yourself, close to the event, in sequence, with times and attribution — describe what was observed rather than what it meant. Never let a draft touch anything evidential, anything describing individuals, or any account involving force, arrest or refusal. Then read each client summary as the client will: does it tell them what actually happened on their site?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write security incident reports?

It should not. Incident narratives are evidential — what the officer observed, in sequence, with times. Generated text tidies sequences and adds detail, which are exactly the wrong changes if the report is examined later.

Can it help with daily shift logs?

Yes. A fixed structure makes reports consistent across officers and makes missing patrols obvious. That consistency is the main value.

What about descriptions of people?

Written by the observing officer, attributed, and exact. Generated description adds characteristics that did not come from anyone.

Is it useful for post orders?

Very. Written once per site in plain language and kept identical between office and site copies prevents the versions drifting apart.

What is the key distinction?

Routine reports value consistency, which tools help with. Incident reports value precision, which tools undermine. Treat them as different document types.
