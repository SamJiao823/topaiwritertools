Parents read daycare text the way a lawyer reads a contract. Every sentence gets interpreted at 6pm when someone is late. So the writing job here is not marketing — it is making the rules unambiguous and the daily life warm.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Handbook sections. Hours, holidays, late pickup, dress code, what to pack. Repetitive, factual, permanently in force.
  • Enrollment and waiting-list emails. The same three messages to every family, with a name swapped in.
  • Daily report templates. Meals, naps, activities, the one sentence a parent actually wants. A model is excellent at a consistent shape.
  • Staff postings. Required certifications, schedule expectations, the bottom pay you will offer.
  • Parent education short posts. Reading with a four-year-old, what a tantrum at pickup means. Educational, warm, low-risk.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Child-safety language. Staff-to-child ratios, supervision language, any statement about how children are protected. Those belong in your licence filing, not a chat window.
  • Medical and allergy instructions. EpiPen timing, epinephrine permission forms, medication logs. Exactly the text a model must not generate.
  • Incident communication. A generated apology after a scrape reads like an insurance form, and parents can tell. The useful sentence names what happened, what you did, and who to call.
  • Promising developmental outcomes. "We prepare children for kindergarten" is fine as aspiration. "We guarantee literacy by five" is a claim you will be held to.
  • Holiday and closure dates. Wrong dates generate angry calls. These are facts, not prose.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

In our 30-day test the low-cost dedicated writer needed about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words, versus roughly 8 minutes on the careful assistant — around $7 per piece at $30/hour. For a daycare operator the money argument is weak again. The real gain is the handbook nobody wants to write and the daily report that takes eleven minutes every afternoon.

A workflow that holds up

Write the handbook once, in sections, and version it in a shared file. Generate each section as you update it — reopened after holidays, new pickup rule — rather than starting over. Parents appreciate a handbook that matches the current year, which nobody has time to type.

For daily reports, fix the shape first: meals, naps, one activity, one sentence of development. Generate the sentence from the day's notes and keep the facts in your own hand. Teachers will not type prose; they will fill a checklist.

Finally, for anything involving an injury, an allergy or a pickup problem, write it yourself in three lines: what happened, what we did, who to contact. Parents need orientation, not polish.

Three facts and no apology template: what happened, what we did, who to call. That is all a parent needs.
