How do I set up a Writesonic account and pick the right plan?

Start at writesonic.com and sign up with Google — it's the fastest path and it's what we did. The free plan exists, but it gates the SEO tools behind the paid tiers, which is the whole reason you'd use Writesonic over a plain chatbot. So plan to pay.

On the signup screen you'll land in a flow that asks what you'll use the tool for. Pick "SEO & Content Writing." Writesonic then routes you to the Freelancer tier, which runs about $20/month billed annually. That tier includes the Article AI long-form editor plus the SEO workspace. One note from our test: the annual price drops notably vs. monthly, so if you're committed, bill annually on day one rather than paying month-to-month for a few cycles first.

How do I use Writesonic's keyword research tools?

This is step one of every article we published during the test. From the dashboard, open the SEO tools section in the left sidebar and click Keyword Research. (Screenshot caption: it's the second card down on the left, with a magnifying-glass icon.)

  1. Enter a seed keyword in the search box. For us, that was usually something like "AI writing tools" or "best email copy tool."
  2. Pick a country and language. Don't skip this — defaulting to "all countries" gives you meaningless volume numbers.
  3. Hit run. Writesonic returns keyword clusters grouped by intent, with search volume and difficulty estimates.
  4. Sort by difficulty and look for targets below 30. Those are the pages you can actually rank for in the first six months. We made this a hard rule after one wasted week chasing a difficulty-60 term.

One honest note: the volume and difficulty numbers are Writesonic's own estimates, and they skew a bit optimistic compared to a dedicated SEO suite. Good enough to pick a target; not good enough to build a full content plan on.

How do I generate an article outline in Writesonic?

Once you have a keyword, go to Articles → Write Article and paste it in. The screen that opens asks for tone, audience, and the number of sections — set these before generating, because changing them later re-rolls the outline.

Before you click generate, click into the SERP Analyzer tab next to the prompt box. It lists the top-ranking pages for your keyword and, usefully, a summary of what each one covers. Do this every time. The outline Writesonic produces is fine, but the SERP Analyzer is what turns a generic outline into one that actually fills gaps your competitors left open. We added an average of two sections per article that way — sections the top results were missing entirely.

Review the generated headings, reorder the weak ones, and cut anything that smells like filler. Then save it as the article draft.

How do I write the article section by section?

Here's the workflow difference that matters. Don't hit "Generate Article" and let it blast out 1,500 words in one go. The one-shot output reads like a brochure — every sentence is technically correct and nothing is memorable. Instead, work section by section:

  1. Click on each outline heading and use the Expand or section-write action to draft just that block.
  2. After each block, stop and rewrite the first and last sentences yourself. Those are the ones readers notice, and they're where Writesonic is weakest.
  3. Paste in your own data, examples, or screenshots as you go. An article with your test numbers in it will always outrank an identical one full of generic claims.

This per-section flow took us about as long as drafting in ChatGPT, but it removed the separate research step entirely — the keyword, outline, and SERP notes were all already in the tab. That's the time saver, and it compounds across every article.

How do I edit and optimize the draft with the SEO checker?

When the draft is written, open the Content Score panel in the article editor. It grades the piece on keyword placement, heading structure, readability, and internal-link coverage, then flags the specific lines to fix. Treat it as a checklist, not a verdict: we found the recommendations useful and occasionally wrong, so we applied the ones that made sense and ignored the rest.

Before you consider it done, read it aloud. This sounds silly, but it catches the two tells of AI writing — the run-on sentence and the sentence that states the obvious. If you'd be embarrassed to see it under your name, rewrite it. The SEO checker fixes search visibility; it can't fix voice.

How do I export and publish?

Writesonic exports to Google Docs, WordPress, and plain HTML. We wrote in the editor, copied to a WordPress draft, and published from there. Two details from our test:

  • Export to Google Docs if you need a human editor in the loop — the formatting survives better than copying the raw text.
  • After publishing, set up the target keyword in AI Search Tracking. It polls the SERPs for that term and shows you movement on a simple chart. We checked it once a week, which beat manually Googling the phrase like we did with earlier tools.

If you're juggling a few articles a week, this whole loop — research to publish — is where Writesonic earns its $20 back. Here's what our timing test looked like across 12 articles:

Where Writesonic still loses to ChatGPT and Claude

We have to be blunt here, because it's the question everyone asks. For raw prose quality, Writesonic does not beat ChatGPT or Claude. The default voice is flat, and it leans on formulas — "in today's fast-paced world" and friends — unless you actively rewrite. In our 2026 benchmark of AI writing tools, Writesonic finished behind both on originality and readability of unedited output.

So the honest mental model is: use Writesonic for the pipeline, use a general LLM for the sentences. Plenty of our test articles went from Writesonic's outline and research into a Claude rewrite of the opening sections. That's not a hack — it's the correct way to use the tool.

When Writesonic isn't worth it

For all its strengths, there are two scenarios where we'd tell you to skip it:

  • You only write short copy. Ad headlines, product descriptions, social captions — Writesonic's long-form editor and SEO tools are overkill, and you're paying for them anyway. For pure short copy, Rytr is cheaper and simpler, and it does the job fine.
  • You already pay for ChatGPT Plus and don't want another subscription. If you're comfortable doing your research with free tools and only need drafting help, ChatGPT Plus covers the writing and you skip the extra bill. You just lose the integrated research.

Writesonic or Jasper — which should you learn first?

We get this a lot because both tools target the same buyer. Jasper is the polished, premium option with mature brand-voice controls and a nicer long-form editor — at roughly double the price. Writesonic wins on the two things budget-conscious content teams care about: native SEO tools and price. In our side-by-side, we'd tell a solo writer to learn Writesonic first and only move to Jasper once brand-voice consistency across many documents becomes the bottleneck. Is Writesonic worth $20 a month in 2026?

For us, yes — conditionally. If you publish SEO content regularly and you'd otherwise stitch together a free keyword tool, a manual SERP review, and a chatbot, Writesonic at $20/month replaces that entire stack with one dashboard. We kept our subscription after the test. If you're a short-copy writer or already on ChatGPT Plus, the value math collapses and you should skip it.
