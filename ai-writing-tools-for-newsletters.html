How We Tested These Tools for 30 Days

We run an independent newsletter, so this was a working test rather than a lab demo. Over 30 days in July–August 2026, we produced 12 newsletters covering product roundups, tool tips, and personal essays. For each one we used an AI tool for three jobs: choosing an angle, drafting subject lines, and writing the body. We tracked time from blank page to final draft, how much rewriting we had to do, and how the finished piece read out loud.

  • Speed: minutes from blank page to a usable draft, per tool.
  • Editing load: how much of the AI output survived after our rewrite.

  • Consistency: whether the tool kept our voice issue after issue, not just once.

Our Tool-by-Tool Breakdown

Writesonic — Strong All-Rounder with Newsletter Templates

Writesonic is the only tool in this list with a true newsletter template, which makes it the easiest place to start. It gives you a structure — subject line, preview text, intro, sections, sign-off — so you're never staring at an empty box. In our tests, the template cut drafting time by roughly 40% compared with starting from a blank chat window. The trade-off: output can feel templated if you don't feed it a specific angle first, and the paid plan is a step up in price from Rytr.

Rytr — Budget Pick for Short Newsletter Content

Rytr is the value pick for short newsletter content. At a fraction of Writesonic's price, it nails subject lines, preview text, and short intros. We generated a batch of five subject lines and usually found one strong option in under a minute. Its weakness is long-form: for full newsletter bodies it runs out of steam and needs more rewriting. If your newsletter is mostly short and punchy, Rytr is hard to beat on price.

ChatGPT — Suited for Editorial Freedom

ChatGPT gives you the most freedom. No templates, no constraints — you can keep your own voice and structure, which is exactly what you want if your newsletter is personal. It's also the strongest at understanding context: feed it past issues and it mimics your tone surprisingly well. The cost is effort: you build the prompt structure yourself, and there's no brand-voice feature unless you set up a custom GPT. For writers who already think in outlines, ChatGPT is the strongest pick.

Grammarly — Suited for Proofreading Before You Hit Send

Grammarly isn't a generator, but it belongs in a newsletter stack because it's the fastest way to catch tone drift, passive voice, and typos before a mistake reaches thousands of inboxes. In our test, every one of the 12 newsletters had at least three fixes from Grammarly that we'd have missed by eye. The free tier covers the basics; Premium's tone and consistency suggestions are worth it if you publish weekly or more.

Jasper — Worth a Look for Teams

If you publish a lot of long-form newsletter content and need a consistent brand voice across a team, Jasper is worth considering. It's the most expensive tool here, but its Brand Voice and workflow features are genuinely strong for that use case.

A 5-Step AI Newsletter Workflow That Works

After 30 days, this is the loop that produced the strongest results with the least friction. It works with any of the generators above.

  1. Lock the angle before you open a tool. One reader, one outcome. No AI can pick your angle for you — and asking it to usually produces generic fluff.
  2. Generate subject lines first. Ask for 3–5 options, then choose or combine. It's the highest-leverage copy in the whole newsletter.
  3. Write the body in blocks, not one prompt. Generate the intro, then each section, then the sign-off. Steering is far easier than fixing one giant blob.
  4. Proofread with a second tool, then cut. Run Grammarly and remove roughly 20% of what's left. Tighter beats longer, every issue.
  5. Feed results back. Track opens, replies, and unsubscribes. Reuse what worked in your next prompts — angles and subject lines compound.

Which Tool Should You Choose?

Pick Writesonic if you want the fastest path from empty page to a finished-looking draft. Pick Rytr if you mostly need subject lines and short sections on a budget. Pick ChatGPT if your newsletter is personal and you want full control. Keep Grammarly in the loop regardless. You don't have to choose just one — most of the strongest newsletter writers we know pair a generator with Grammarly. And a strong AI for newsletter content isn't the most expensive one; it's the tool you'll actually open and edit every week.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose an AI writing tool for newsletters?

Writesonic is a strong all-rounder thanks to its built-in newsletter template; Rytr is the budget pick for short content.

Can AI write my whole newsletter for me?

It can draft, but the strongest results come from a real angle and heavy editing — AI helps most with subject lines, structure, and getting past a blank page.

Is ChatGPT good for writing newsletters?

Yes, for flexibility and voice control — but it has no templates, so you have to structure the prompts yourself.

How much do AI newsletter writing tools cost?

Free tiers exist for Rytr, ChatGPT, and Grammarly. Paid plans run roughly $7.50–$20 per month for the tools in this guide, with premium tools like Jasper costing more.

Should I use AI for newsletter subject lines?

Yes — generating 3–5 options and picking a tool is one of the highest-leverage uses of AI in a newsletter workflow.
