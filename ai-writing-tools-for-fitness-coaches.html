Coaching is text-heavy. Eleven clients, the same four questions every week, and a feed that has to keep posting. The repetition is where a writing tool earns its keep — and where it becomes dangerous if you let it write the training.

Safe to automate

  • Weekly check-in templates. Weight, sleep, training days, soreness, one sentence on progress. Ask for a numbered list, fill in the numbers yourself.
  • Class and session descriptions. Level, duration, what to bring. Reused hundreds of times a year.
  • Long-form lead magnets. The "first 30 days at home" guide is a long document written once and sold forever.
  • Objection replies. "I don't have time", "I'm not fit enough", "I've tried apps". Answered the same way every time.
  • Follow-up sequences for leads. Five emails, written once, sent for years.

Do not let it write these

  • Anything about a specific client's body. Loads, reps, restrictions, contraindications. A model does not know that client's shoulder, their history, or their physiotherapist's note.
  • Injury or medical wording. "Rebuild your knee in six weeks" is a claim no coach can make about a person they have not assessed.
  • Results promises. Before-and-after statements belong to the client, in the client's voice, with the client's permission.
  • Calorie or supplement numbers. Those come from your nutrition software or a registered dietitian, not from a language model.

Cost of your time, measured

In our 30-day benchmark the cheap dedicated writer took about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words while the premium tool took about 8 minutes — roughly $7 per piece at a $30/hour rate. For a coach sending 40 check-ins and one guide a month, that is hours back, not dollars.

The workflow that keeps clients trusting you

Keep a client facts block: goal, current load, injuries, availability, last session note. Feed it each time you draft a check-in. The tool then references the real plan, and you only fix the tone. Save the versions that got a reply and reuse them as the examples in your next prompt.

The one line never to outsource is the answer to "is this normal?" A client asking that needs the person who knows their history, not a generator.

What coaches ask

Can AI write my workout plans?

It can draft a template structure for a client group. Individual programming stays yours — the model has no idea what that person can lift today.

Is it fine to use AI for client check-ins?

Yes, if the numbers are yours. Clients notice when a sentence sounds generic; adding one specific line per person fixes it.

Should I use a paid tool or ChatGPT?

If you also publish class pages and guides that bring in search traffic, compare the paid options. If it is only DMs, a free assistant does the same job.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write workout plans for clients?

As a draft structure for groups. Individual programming must come from your assessment of that person.

Is it safe to send AI-written check-ins?
