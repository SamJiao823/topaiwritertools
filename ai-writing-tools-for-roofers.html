Roofing is a trade where the paperwork decides the payment. Two roofers can do identical work, and the one whose inspection notes documented the damage specifically, with dated photographs described in writing, gets paid properly. Generated text tends to smooth over exactly the specifics that matter — a damaged area becomes "some storm wear", and the claim gets adjusted down.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Estimate structure. Scope by roof face, materials by line item, exclusions stated plainly, validity window. Applied identically every time.
  • Inspection report templates. The recurring headings — areas inspected, findings per slope, materials condition, flashing, penetrations, drainage — with the findings filled in by you.
  • Storm damage summaries. Turning field notes into a consistent narrative that an adjuster can follow, with each item tied to a location and a date.
  • Customer follow-ups after storms. Reassurance, what to document, and how to get an inspection without pressure tactics.
  • Maintenance reminder programmes. Seasonal checks, gutter clearing, post-storm inspections. Written once, sent for years.
  • Financing explainers. Plain-language description of how payment plans generally work, kept free of specific terms.
  • Review requests. One message after completion, once.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Insurance correspondence. A letter to an insurer describing damage is part of a claim. A generated sentence that overstates or understates can create real problems for the homeowner and for you.
  • Warranty and guarantee wording. Duration, what is covered, what voids it. Generated warranty language is consistently more generous than the actual warranty.
  • Measurement and scope figures. Squares, square footage, pitch, layer counts. Every figure from your measurement, never from a draft.
  • Lifespan claims. "Twenty-year roof" applied to a material based on nothing. Service life depends on the product, the install and the climate.
  • Code and permit statements. What is required, what was permitted, what was inspected. Write these from your own knowledge of the local requirement.
  • Emergency promises. "Same-day tarping" or a response window nobody committed to, which is how a storm week goes badly.

Notes and photographs: what makes a claim defensible

An adjuster is not adversarial by default, but they are reading for specificity, and specificity is what generated prose removes. Three habits make documentation hold up. Every finding gets a location and a date — three missing shingles at the south-west ridge, wind-lifted, photographed 14 March — rather than "some storm damage to the roof". Describe mechanism as observed, not as inferred: "granule loss consistent with hail impact" is a claim; "granule loss at these six locations, with the adjacent fascia also dented" is an observation that lets someone else draw the conclusion. Reference the photograph in the text, so the two documents corroborate each other. None of this is writing work to delegate — it is the difference between being credible and being arguable.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a roofing crew the saving is real but lands in structure, not substance: the estimates and reports get consistent, while the findings still have to be written by whoever was on the roof.

A workflow that holds up

Write field notes on site, before you leave — specific location, specific problem, photograph taken. Those notes are the source; everything downstream refers back to them. Generate the report and estimate structure from a fixed template so nothing gets omitted between jobs, and fill in every finding and figure yourself. Keep insurance letters short, factual and tied to documented findings, because speculation in writing is what gets quotes and claims argued. Write the warranty section once, from your actual written warranty, and reuse it verbatim on every job. Then read each estimate as the adjuster will read it: is every claimed item tied to a specific location and a dated observation?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write roof inspection reports?

It can produce a consistent template with every heading in the right order. The findings, measurements and observations have to come from whoever was on the roof.

Is it safe for insurance claim letters?

Keep them factual and tied to documented findings with dates and locations. Speculation or generous phrasing in a claim letter creates problems for everyone involved.

What generated wording causes the most trouble?

Warranty language. Generated warranty and guarantee statements are consistently broader than the actual written warranty, and that difference surfaces later.

What should a small crew set up first?

The estimate template with explicit exclusions, and the inspection report headings. Both repeat on every job and consistency is what makes them defensible.
