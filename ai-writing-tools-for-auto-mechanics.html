Auto writing is a trust document. The customer is buying an explanation as much as a repair. AI can produce the explanation reliably. It cannot stand behind the warranty clause.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Service menu descriptions. Oil, brake, timing belt, coolant flush, inspection. Identical every week.
  • Maintenance reminders. The letter that turns a one-time customer into a recurring one. Short, factual, dated.
  • Diagnostic explanations. What the code means, what we found, what we recommend and what we would not touch.
  • Post-repair summaries. Parts replaced, what to watch, when to come back.
  • Review replies. Thanks, and a route back to the phone. No defending a diagnosis in public.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Warranty language. Parts and labour warranty wording is regulated in many states and is a contract. That paragraph is yours, written and approved by someone who knows your jurisdiction.
  • Safety claims. "Your brakes are safe again" is a statement about a system under load. Do not let a model sign off on safety.
  • Part numbers and specs. It will name a part with a confident description. You are now liable for that part fitting.
  • Price guarantees. "We guarantee the lowest price" is a promise you must be prepared to honour in writing.
  • Diagnostic certainty. A model writing "the cause is the cam sensor" is exactly the kind of sentence a customer repeats back at you in an argument.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

In our 30-day test the low-cost dedicated writer needed about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words versus roughly 8 minutes on the careful assistant — around $7 per piece at $30/hour. A shop billing $90 to $180 an hour will not be moved by that. The moved number is the follow-up email nobody sends because closing time comes too fast.

A workflow that holds up

Build a repair-note template with four fixed sections: what the customer reported, what we found, what we did, what we recommend next. Generate the language, fill the facts from the work order. That template alone prevents most "you didn't tell me" conversations.

Keep the warranty paragraph locked in a separate document that you never let a model rewrite. It is the one piece of text in the shop that has to be exactly right.

And when explaining a diagnostic, say what you measured. Customers accept a long explanation far more easily when it contains a number and a part name than when it contains a conclusion.
