Planners do not suffer from a lack of ideas, they suffer from documents. A run-of-show, a supplier brief, a sponsor thank-you, a post-event recap with numbers in it. All templates. All rewritten for the fifteenth time this month.

Worth automating

  • Run-of-show and schedules. Ask for a table with time, owner, load-in, notes. You fill the times; it formats.
  • Supplier and vendor briefs. Deliverables, times, contacts, access notes. Repetitive and boring — ideal.
  • Registration and venue pages. What to bring, what time, where to park, what is included. Written once, edited yearly.
  • Sponsor asks. The same email with a new audience stat. Keep a stats block and paste it every time.
  • Post-event recaps. Attendance, what broke, what worked. Perfect for a model because the facts arrive as a list.

Where it breaks

  • Any number that has to be exact. Capacities, timings, catering counts, insurance limits. If the wrong number puts 40 people in a room built for 30, that is your fault, not the model's.
  • Legal and insurance wording. Contracts with venues, performers and suppliers stay on a lawyer's template.
  • Creative direction. The theme, the vibe, the thing people will photograph. A model produces the average of every event ever planned.
  • The apology email after a failure. That one carries the next year's bookings. Write it yourself, own it in the first sentence.

Editing time, from our 30-day test

Our testing found roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the cheap dedicated writer versus 8 minutes on the premium tool — about $7 per piece of your time at $30/hour. Event work is high volume and low word count, so the real saving is not per word, it is the two hours of formatting you get back every event.

Set it up once, use it forever

Keep a reusable kit file: your standard timings, supplier contacts, insurance line, parking details, accessibility notes, logo rules, and the last event's stats. Every prompt starts with it. You stop explaining your own business each time, and nobody receives a brief that invents their load-in window.

Then hand-write exactly one thing per event: the sentence in the thank-you email that mentions what went wrong and what you changed. That is the line a returning client remembers.
