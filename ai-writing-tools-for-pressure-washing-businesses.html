Almost all the writing in this trade sits at two moments: the quote and the follow-up. Both are repetitive, both are read by people comparing you against two other companies, and both are where small wording choices change the outcome. That makes the category a reasonable fit for generated drafts — with one significant exception.

Where it clearly helped

Quote descriptions that separate the work from the price

Quotes had previously listed a service and a number. Drafting them with three labelled parts — what we will clean, what we will use and protect, and what affects the price — reduced the back-and-forth noticeably. Stating what is excluded helped most: "does not include interior windows" or "assumes access to an outdoor tap" removed the most common follow-up question.

Service pages for each surface type

Driveways, siding, decks, roofs and commercial facades each need different explanation — pressure levels, what can be damaged, how long it takes. Generating one page per surface was fast, and the factual content is checkable against equipment specifications.

Review requests

The pattern that worked: ask once, at the end of the job, in one sentence with the link. Generated drafts reliably over-asked — adding thanks, reminders and a second ask — which reads as pressure. Shorten them to a single request.

Where it nearly caused problems

Damage disclaimers

This was the real issue. Generated text described surfaces as safe to clean, and mentioned pressure levels, with confidence it had no basis for. Pressure washing damages things — soft wood, aged mortar, painted surfaces, certain sidings — and a document asserting a surface is safe becomes a problem the moment something goes wrong. Surface assessment is the operator's judgement on the day.

Results descriptions

Generated copy promised outcomes ("restored to like-new") rather than describing the process. Promises about results are commitments; descriptions of method are not.

Chemical and runoff statements

Drafts included statements about cleaning agents and environmental handling. Both are regulated, vary by location, and should come from your own product documentation and local requirements.

Where most disputes actually start

Looking back over a season of jobs, nearly every disagreement traced to something not said before work began, rather than to the work itself. Three gaps recur. Pre-existing condition — cracked render, lifted sealant, oxidised paint, loose pointing — often only becomes visible once the surface is wet and clean, at which point it reads as fresh damage. Photographs before starting, in daylight, cost minutes and settle it. Water supply: a job quoted assuming an outdoor tap takes considerably longer without one, and the difference is yours unless it was stated in advance. Run-off and neighbouring property — where the water goes and what it carries deserves a sentence in writing.

The other document that earns its keep is the follow-up after a first clean: what was treated, what will likely return and roughly when, and what the customer can do in between. It is short, it is the reason people book a repeat, and it is the message most operators never send.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

A quote that took about 15 minutes to write manually took roughly 5 minutes to draft and review, near $5 saved at $30 per hour. Service pages dropped from about 50 minutes to around 18 minutes. Review requests were the exception: generated versions needed more editing than writing them took, because the default output was too long to send.

A workflow that holds up

Keep one quote template with the three labelled parts and the exclusions written in. Generate surface pages from your own equipment specifications and review each against the manual. Send review requests as a single sentence with the link, the same day. Leave surface assessment, chemical handling and runoff statements entirely to the operator — those are judgement and compliance, not writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write my quotes?

The scope description, yes, from a template you control. The diagnosis of what a surface needs has to come from seeing it.

Should I use it for damage disclaimers?

No. Asserting a surface is safe to clean is a judgement call with consequences. Assess in person and document what you observed.

Can it write review requests?

Yes, but shorten the draft. Generated versions tend to say too much; one sentence with the link performs better.

What about chemical and runoff statements?

Use your own product documentation and check local requirements. This varies by location and is regulated.

Does it help with seasonal marketing?

Yes — reminders for spring cleaning, gutter season and post-storm work are repetitive and easy to verify.

How much time does it really save?

About 10 to 30 minutes per piece in our testing, concentrated in quotes and service pages.
