Three document types carried nearly all of the value: quote letters, pre-install instructions, and change orders. A fourth — anything touching property boundaries — turned out to be where the risk concentrated, and it is the kind of risk that produces a complaint rather than a correction.

The pattern is familiar from other trades. Documents that repeat with different variables are worth generating. Documents that carry legal or positional weight are not, and fencing has more of those than most trades, because a fence sits on a line between two properties.

Where it helped

Quote letters

A fencing quote has a fixed shape — material, linear feet, height, gate count, terrain adjustment, timeline — and only the numbers change between jobs. Feeding that structure in once and generating the letter for each job cut drafting from around 18 minutes to 5. The important part was the exclusion list: old fence removal, permit fees, utility locates, and rock or root conditions each stated separately. Quotes that failed did so because they were silent on what happened if the ground turned out to be harder than it looked.

Pre-install instructions

The message sent a few days before arrival — clear the fence line, mark sprinkler heads, secure pets, unlock the gate, confirm the property corners. Generated versions were consistently better than what we had been sending, mostly because the model remembered items a person forgets under time pressure. This is the highest-value document in the set, because a single missed item costs a return trip.

Change orders

When the scope shifts — an extra gate, a grade change, a switch from wood to vinyl — the change order needs to state what changed, what it costs, and what it does to the completion date. The generated structure was sound and took about 4 minutes to adapt versus roughly 15 to draft. The one rule we kept: never let a generated change order describe the reason for the change without reviewing it, since that reason becomes part of the record.

Where it failed

Property lines and boundary statements

Drafts confidently stated where the property line was, or asserted that a fence would be built "on your side of the boundary". Nobody working from a language model can establish that. It needs a survey, an existing monument, or an agreement between neighbours, and the written document should say the boundary was confirmed by one of those methods rather than describing it as fact. We now treat every boundary sentence as requiring a person.

Utility locates

Generated text referred to calling 811 or a local equivalent, sometimes with the wrong number for the region and sometimes as though the call had already happened. Whether a locate has been requested is a fact about the job, not something to draft. We split it into a mandatory field: locate requested on [date], ticket number [number]. If the field is empty, the document does not go out.

HOA and local code specifics

Drafts described setback rules, height limits and permitted materials with specifics that were plausible and unverified — and these vary not just by state but by subdivision. Any sentence naming a numeric limit now requires a citation to the actual rule, or it becomes "confirm with your HOA before we schedule".

Grade and soil conditions

Text assumed level ground or standard digging conditions. On a sloped lot or in rocky soil, that assumption is the difference between a quote and an argument. The pricing model and the document both need a terrain field, filled in after a site visit rather than generated.

What customers actually read

A homeowner comparing two fencing quotes rarely reads the whole document — they look at the total, the material, and the timeline, then scan for anything that looks like it could cost more later. The exclusion list is doing more work than the sales copy. In our testing, quotes with a clearly separated "not included" section drew fewer follow-up questions than longer quotes that buried the same information in prose.

One addition worth making: a short paragraph on what happens on the day — arrival window, how long it takes, whether someone needs to be home, what happens to the old fence. It answers the questions people would otherwise phone about.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

A quote letter that took about 18 minutes came down to roughly 5 minutes, near $7 saved at $30 per hour. Pre-install instructions went from around 12 minutes to 3. Change orders improved by about 11 minutes each. Boundary-related content showed no reliable saving, since it required verification regardless.

A workflow that holds up

Template the quote letter with mandatory fields for terrain, old fence removal and permit responsibility. Send pre-install instructions automatically once the date is confirmed. Generate change orders from a fixed structure and review the reason text yourself. Property line statements, utility locate status and code specifics come from your own verification — checked on site, recorded in the job file, and written by whoever is accountable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write our fencing quotes?

The letter, yes — with mandatory fields for terrain, removals and permits filled in from the site visit. The numbers and exclusions stay yours.

Should it describe the property line?

No. A boundary needs a survey or a confirmed monument. Record how it was established instead.

What about utility locates?

Treat as a status field with a date and ticket number, never generated text.

Can it handle HOA rules?

Only as a prompt to confirm. Height limits and materials vary by subdivision.

What about warranties?

Template the structure, then confirm the actual terms match what you offer before sending.

Is this worth it for a two-person crew?

In our testing the pre-install instructions alone paid for the subscription by avoiding return trips.
