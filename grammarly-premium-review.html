Grammarly Premium Review 2026: What the Rename to "Pro" Actually Changes

📝 Independent review by TopAIWritingTools.com · Published August 4, 2026 · Updated September 16, 2026
Short answer: Grammarly no longer sells a plan called "Premium" — the paid tier was renamed Pro, and this page's URL keeps the old name because that is how it has been indexed since August 2026. Pro is $30/mo billed monthly or $12/mo billed annually ($144/year). The gap between those two numbers is the single most important thing to understand before you subscribe: the same plan costs 150% more if you pay monthly. Grammarly is not in our 30-day benchmark — it edits text rather than generating it, so we publish no generation score for it, and this page carries no score. Everything below is based on publicly available plan information, and we say so wherever a number could not be confirmed.

The Name Change: "Premium" Is Now "Pro"

If you searched for a "Grammarly Premium review", you are looking for a product that Grammarly has since renamed. The paid consumer tier that used to be called Premium is now sold as Grammarly Pro. The free tier is still just Grammarly (sometimes written "Grammarly Free"), and the team tier is still Grammarly Business.

We are keeping this page at /grammarly-premium-review rather than moving it, because the URL has been live and indexed since August 4, 2026 and renaming it would break existing links for no reader benefit. What matters is that the content is not stale: wherever older guides on the web — including older versions of this one — say "Premium", read "Pro". If a page still describes Premium as a current plan name, that page has not been updated.

The rename did not change the underlying product line. It is a naming change, not a new tier, and it is not a signal that a new plan sits above or below it. Anyone quoting a "Grammarly Premium" price in 2026 is quoting the current Pro price under an old label, which is harmless — but anyone claiming Premium and Pro are two different plans is wrong.

Grammarly Pricing in 2026: Free, Pro, and Business

Grammarly runs three tiers. Every price below is a September 2026 snapshot, and each one is labelled with its billing cycle, because Grammarly charges materially different amounts depending on whether you pay monthly or annually.

First, Pro and Business are both billed annually at the prices shown — the $12/mo and $15/user/mo figures are annual-plan rates, not month-to-month rates. Second, the monthly Pro rate ($30) and the annual Pro rate ($12) describe the same product. There is no feature difference; the only variable is how you pay.

The Billing Trap: $30/Month vs $12/Month for the Same Plan

💰 The arithmetic

Twelve months at the monthly rate costs $360 ($30 × 12). The annual plan costs $144. Choosing monthly costs $216 more per year — a 150% premium for the privilege of not paying upfront.

Put differently: $30/mo is 2.5× the annual effective rate of $12/mo. This is not a Grammarly-specific trick — most subscription software prices this way — but the size of the gap here is large enough that it deserves to be stated plainly rather than buried in fine print.

When does the annual plan pay for itself?

Divide the annual fee by the monthly fee: $144 ÷ $30 = 4.8 months. That means the annual charge equals roughly five months of monthly payments. If you keep the subscription for more than about five months, the annual plan has already cost you less than paying month to month would have; every month past that is pure saving, up to $216 across a year. If you are confident you will use it for at least half a year, annual wins. If you are testing whether Grammarly fits your workflow at all, monthly is the honest way to find out — you pay a premium for the option to leave, and for a short test that premium is small.

Be clear-eyed about the risk on the other side: annual means $144 leaves your account on day one. If you stop writing in month two, monthly would have cost you $60 and annual cost you $144. The annual plan is a bet that you will still be using it in the second half of the year.

What Pro Adds Over Free

The free tier is not a trial — it is a permanently free product, and for a lot of people it is genuinely enough. The honest framing is that Free handles correctness and Pro handles consistency and register, which are different jobs.

Consistency checks (spelling, punctuation, formatting)NoYes

Drafts long-form content from scratchNoNo

The last row is the one people get wrong. Grammarly is an editor. It will suggest a better sentence, flag a harsh tone, or catch a style inconsistency across a 3,000-word document — it will not write the document. If drafting is what you need, Grammarly belongs in the stack after a drafting tool, not instead of one. Our monthly cost breakdown covers what that two-tool stack actually costs.

Note the free tier's 100 AI prompts per month: that is a real allowance, not a demo. For someone who uses AI rewriting a handful of times a week, Free is a working product, and the upgrade case has to rest on consistency checks, plagiarism detection, and tone work rather than on basic correction.

Who Should Pay for Grammarly Pro

Pro earns its price for a fairly specific set of people. The pattern in all of them is the same: they write enough that sentence-level quality compounds, and their writing goes in front of people who notice.

  • Non-native English writers. This is the strongest case. The value is not just error correction — it is the tone and fluency layer, which is the part that is hardest to self-edit and the part free tools handle least well.
  • People who publish daily and cannot afford an embarrassing typo. Client email, proposals, published articles, LinkedIn posts under your own name. When a single error has a reputational cost, $12/mo is cheap insurance.
  • Anyone maintaining a long document. Consistency checking is the underrated Pro feature. A 40-page report that spells the same term three different ways is a credibility problem, and it is the kind of problem human proofreading misses.
  • Teams with a shared standard. That is the Business tier at $15/user/mo (annual), where the argument is administration and shared style rather than individual correction.

Who Should Not Pay

  • Casual writers. If you write a few emails a week, the free tier covers it. There is no shame in staying on Free — it is a permanent product, not a nagware trial.
  • People who need a drafter, not an editor. If the problem is a blank page, a grammar tool does not solve it. A drafting tool plus free-tier polishing is the cheaper path, and we cover the options in free AI writing tools.
  • Fiction and creative writers who want voice preserved. Aggressive correctness suggestions can flatten deliberate stylistic choices. That is a known trade-off of any rules-driven editor, and it is the most common complaint we hear about this class of tool.
  • Anyone who will not use it for six months. Given the $144 upfront, the monthly plan is the rational test — and if you are unsure, you are exactly the person the annual discount is priced against.

Grammarly Business at $15/User/Month (Annual)

Business is billed annually at $15 per user per month. Do not compare that number directly to Pro's $12/mo annual rate and conclude Business is only $3 more — the comparison that matters is whether you need seat administration and a shared standard at all. For a solo writer, Business is a worse deal than Pro. For a team of ten that wants one style guide enforced across everyone's output, it is a different product category and $15/user/mo is not unreasonable.

The same billing logic applies here as everywhere else: $15/user/mo is the annual rate. Per-seat monthly pricing, where it is offered, runs higher. Confirm the current figure on Grammarly's own pricing page before you commit budget to it.

Verdict

Grammarly Pro is a good product with an unusually punishing billing structure, and those two facts are independent. The product is the best-known sentence-level editor available, and the Pro tier adds the consistency and tone layers that the free tier deliberately withholds. The billing structure means the same plan costs $144/year or $360/year depending on a single checkbox.

So the decision has two parts. First: do you actually need Pro over Free? If you write professionally, are a non-native English writer, or maintain long documents, yes. Second: if yes, do not pay monthly for more than a few months. Test on monthly if you need to test, then switch to annual once you know you are keeping it — the switch alone is worth $216 a year.

And if drafting is your bottleneck rather than polishing, this is the wrong category of tool. Frequently Asked Questions

Is Grammarly Premium still called Premium?

No. Grammarly renamed the paid consumer tier from Premium to Pro, and this page keeps the old name in its URL only because that address has been indexed since August 2026. Wherever older guides say Premium, read Pro — they describe the same plan.

Is Grammarly Premium worth $30 a month?

At the monthly rate of $30, only if you are testing and expect to stop soon. The same Pro plan costs $12/mo when billed annually ($144/year), so $30/mo is a 150% premium — $216 more across a year. If you will keep it past about five months, the annual plan is the correct choice, and $30/mo should be treated as a short-term rate.

What is the difference between Grammarly Free and Pro?

Free covers basic grammar and spelling correction and includes 100 AI prompts per month. Pro adds tone and clarity suggestions, consistency checks across a whole document, and plagiarism detection. Neither tier drafts content from scratch — Grammarly is an editor, not a writer.

Was Grammarly included in your 30-day blind test?

Grammarly is not in our 30-day benchmark: it edits text rather than generating it, and we publish no generation score for it. The tools in that test are Claude, ChatGPT, Writesonic, Jasper, Rytr, Copy.ai, and Hypotenuse. That is why this page publishes no score for Grammarly — the plan and billing analysis here rests on public information, not on our benchmark data.

Does Grammarly generate content?

No — Grammarly is an editing tool. It improves sentences you have already written but does not produce a draft. If you need drafting, pair it with a generator such as Claude or ChatGPT and use Grammarly as the polish step.
