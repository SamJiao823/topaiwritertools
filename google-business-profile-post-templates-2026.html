Google Business Profile Post Templates: 10 Formats That Get Local Businesses Seen

A Google Business Profile post is 1,500 characters of public real estate that most local businesses leave empty for months. It is also the most constrained writing there is, which makes it a good fit for generation — provided you respect the limits and stop promising things you will not honour.

Nobody reads these posts the way they read a blog. They glance at one while deciding whether to call you, and they take two things from it: that the business is currently operating, and what to do next. That is why most generated posts fail — they are written like announcements, full of context and no instruction.

What actually works in these posts

Four rules held up across our testing. Lead with the concrete thing — the offer, the date, the change — not with your company name. Include one action: call, book, visit, or claim, said once, clearly. Keep it inside about 150 to 300 words; the visible area is small and the rest is truncated. And match the post type to the format Google offers, because the "offer" type has fields — dates, terms, a code — that the plain update does not.

The ten templates

1. Offer

[Discount or package] for [who], until [date]. [What is included, in a list]. Book by [method] or quote [code]. Always include an end date — an undated offer looks stale regardless of when it was posted.

2. New service or product

Now offering [service] at [location]. It solves [specific problem] for [specific customer]. First [number] bookings get [incentive]. One service per post; combining three dilutes all of them.

3. Seasonal hours

[Dates] we are open [hours]. Closed [date] for [reason]. Emergency work still available at [number] if you offer it. This is the post type that prevents angry calls, and it is the one businesses forget.

4. Event

[Event] on [date] at [time]. [What happens, who it suits, cost]. Register by [date] at [link]. Include whether booking is required — that is the question people actually have.

5. Update

[What changed] as of [date]. [Why it matters to a customer]. Nothing you need to do, or: [the one thing they should do]. Good for new equipment, extended coverage, a new team member.

6. Behind the scenes

[Person or team] doing [thing] this week. [One specific detail — the job, the tool, the number]. Same service you know, [slight difference]. Human posts outperform polished ones here.

7. Availability

[Number] slots open [days] for [service]. Usually booked [lead time], so these will go. Call [number] or book at [link]. Scarcity has to be true — say the real number.

8. Hiring

We are hiring [role]. [Honest hardest part of the job]. [Who it suits]. Apply by [method]. This doubles as recruiting content and gets read by people who already know your business.

9. Question and answer

"[The question customers ask most]." Short answer: [answer]. Full answer on [link]. Ask us anything at [number]. This is how local businesses pick up long-tail visibility without writing articles.

10. Community or seasonal tie-in

[Season or local event] means [specific thing for our customers]. We are [doing X] through [date]. [One action]. Keep the tie real; invented relevance is obvious.

What gets posts rejected, and what just fails quietly

Two categories here, and they are different problems. Rejected or taken down: prohibited content, obvious policy violations, misleading offers, and anything with contact details in the image rather than the text. Not rejected, just useless: missing or vague call to action, no end date on an offer, more than about three hashtags, promotional language that reads as advertising rather than information, an image that is mostly text, and — most common — a post written for everyone, which reaches nobody.

What this costs, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. These posts are 150 words, so the per-post cost is small; the real expense is the business owner forgetting to post for eleven weeks. Generating ten in one sitting and scheduling them removes that.

A workflow that holds up

Batch them: generate a month of posts in one session, then check each against limits and dates. Keep a document with your actual facts — hours, phone, services, prices, real availability — and paste the relevant part into the prompt, because a generated price or deadline is worse than no post. Give each post one instruction, not three. Then set a reminder for the ones tied to dates, because an expired offer sitting on a profile suggests the rest of the information is stale too. Reply to the reviews these posts generate while you are there; that visibility compounds more than the posts do.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a Google Business Profile post be?

Around 150 to 300 words. There is room for far more, but the visible area is small and anything past roughly that gets truncated.

Do these posts help local search visibility?

They signal that the profile is active and give people a reason to click through. They are a supporting habit rather than a ranking lever on their own.

Can AI write them safely?

Yes for structure and phrasing. Fill in prices, dates, availability and contact details yourself — a generated deadline or offer that you cannot honour is worse than not posting.

How often should I post?

Once a week is a realistic target for most local businesses, and consistency matters more than frequency. Batch several at once so it actually happens.
