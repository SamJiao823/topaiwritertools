Where it clearly helped

Class descriptions that say what actually happens. Most studio timetables list levels and nothing else, which makes parents guess whether their six-year-old belongs in Primary A. Describing each class — what is taught that term, what they need to bring, prior experience required, whether parents watch — reduced the mismatch enquiries noticeably. It also made the timetable page do work it had never done.

Term and recital logistics. Dates, arrival times, what to wear under costume, hair and makeup requirements, where to drop off and collect, how long it runs, whether tickets include everyone. The volume of individual questions dropped sharply when one document answered all of them in order, because the same twelve questions arrive every year.

Absence and catch-up messages. Short, kind, and sent promptly — lessens the parental guilt spiral and keeps them informed. These are formulaic and repetitive, which suits templating well.

Where it nearly went wrong

Anything about a child. Progress notes, behavioural observations, anything mentioned to one parent about another family's child. These are written by the teacher who saw it, and anything involving safeguarding concerns follows your policy documents rather than anything generated. This is not a caution about tone — it is about records that should be attributable to a person.

Health, injury and physical statements. "Safe for hypermobile students", "suitable during pregnancy", advice about an injury or a medical condition. Nothing here comes from a draft; it comes from the teacher's knowledge, the parent's information, and where relevant a medical professional.

Ability and progression claims. "Your child is ready for the next level" written by a tool that has not watched them dance. Progression decisions are made by the teacher and communicated by them.

Recital season without the chaos

The single document that helps most is the one you send two weeks before: arrival time by class, what they arrive in, where they wait, who collects them and from where, running order with approximate times, and what happens if someone is late. Written once from your own schedule and reused, it removes most of the day-of confusion — and every fact in it has to be correct rather than plausible, which is why it gets written from the actual running order.

Retention is a writing problem

Studios lose students at two predictable points, and neither is about teaching quality. The first is the first half-term, when a parent unsure about progress sees nothing concrete to go on. A short message at week five describing what the class is currently working toward changes that. The second is the gap before re-enrolment, where silence is read as indifference. Over thirty days the studios that wrote to parents on a schedule rather than only when something needed announcing kept noticeably more students through both points. None of it was elaborate — it just existed.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a studio the real return was writing the pages and documents that previously did not exist: full class descriptions, and a complete recital brief.

A workflow that holds up

Write one good description per class, reviewed once a term, covering what is actually taught rather than what sounds appealing. Keep the recital brief as a living document from your real schedule, with dates checked against the venue. Send absence messages promptly and without guilt-inducing language. Anything about a specific child, their progress, their health or their conduct is written by the teacher who observed it — and anything safeguarding-related follows your policy exactly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write dance class descriptions?

Yes, and it is high value — describing what is taught that term, required experience, and what to bring removes most enquiry mismatches.

Can it write about individual students?

No. Progress notes, behaviour observations and anything safeguarding-related are written by the teacher who observed them and follow your policy documents.

What about health or injury advice?

Never generated. Anything about injuries, medical conditions or physical suitability comes from the teacher, the parent, and where relevant a medical professional.

What helps most during recital season?

One complete brief sent two weeks ahead: arrival times by class, what to arrive in, who collects from where, running order, and what happens if someone is late.

What should a studio write first?

Full class descriptions for every level, then a reusable recital brief built from your actual schedule.
