Catering writing has a hard edge that most content does not: it can be a food-safety problem. One wrong word about an ingredient, one allergy note that is too casual, and the document a client keeps becomes evidence. Everything else about the writing — the menu blurb, the tasting invitation, the note about travel distance — is repetition, which is where the tools help.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Menu item descriptions. What is in it, how it is served, what it comes with. Short, factual, written forty times a year.
  • Quote breakdowns. Per head, minimum spend, what is included, travel andstaffing. Explaining the number prevents the price conversation later.
  • Tasting invitations. Polite, logistically specific, and easy to leave unread when it is corporate.
  • Run sheets for event day. Times, load-in, who brings what, contact list, when the first guest arrives. Long, boring, and worth having in one consistent shape.
  • Renewal and follow-up letters. The check-in after a wedding or a quarterly office lunch.
  • Short social posts from one event. A single event description becomes a caption and a follow-up email.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Allergen language. "Contains nuts" when the dish is prepared in a kitchen that also handles nuts, or no note at all because the model treated it as decoration. This is the line that matters most in catering writing.
  • Dietary claims. "Gluten free" or "vegan" used loosely, then a guest cannot eat the meal. Those classifications come from your kitchen process, not from a description.
  • Cross-contamination explanations. Written in a way that promises more safety than the setup delivers. Being honest here costs less than one incident.
  • Ingredient origins and certification labels. Where the produce came from, whether the meat is certified, organic status. Those are supply-chain facts.
  • Storage and handling instructions. What the client must do with hot food at the venue. Getting this wrong is not a copy problem.
  • Headcounts and timing promises. "Arrives fully set up an hour early" when load-in has never once worked that way.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant, close to $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. The value for a caterer is the run sheet and the quote: two documents that are written once, reused all season, and usually rewritten badly each time.

A workflow that holds up

Write the menu and the dietary matrix once, properly, with the kitchen manager in the room, then generate every variation from those two documents. Anything mentioning an ingredient, an allergen or a dietary label should be generated from a file that came out of the kitchen, and a person should confirm the finished text before it reaches a client. For quotes, generate the explanation of the number, then read it aloud as though you were the client who thinks it is too expensive. Event day documents should be generated from a template with the venue fields left blank to fill in yourself, because those fields change every time. Read the menu at a wedding table, in daylight, on paper. The dish you cannot pronounce is the one a guest will ask about.

What caterers ask

Can AI write menu descriptions?

Yes, from a document that lists the real ingredients. A model does not know what goes into your brine.

Is it safe for allergen statements?

Only as a starting shape. The wording should come from your kitchen's process and be confirmed by whoever owns food safety.

Which tool is worth the monthly fee?

The cheap plan for menus and correspondence. A general assistant pays off if you send long run sheets or seasonal menu changes often.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write my menu descriptions?

Yes, from a document listing the real ingredients. A model does not know what goes into your recipes, so every item has to be supplied.

How careful should I be with allergen wording?

Carefully. Generate the shape, then have whoever owns food safety confirm the final text. This is the one area where a vague sentence has consequences.

What should a small caterer automate first?

The quote breakdown and the event run sheet. Both are reused all season and usually rewritten badly each time.
