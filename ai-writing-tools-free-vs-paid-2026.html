"Is the free version enough" is the wrong question, because it depends entirely on which wall you have hit. Four limits show up repeatedly, and they are not the same shape: a monthly word or credit ceiling, a weaker model behind the interface, no memory of your brief across sessions, and restrictions on commercial use or team seats. Paying removes one of them. Paying for the wrong one is how people end up on a $49 plan they use like a $10 one.

What free tiers genuinely cover

More than the pricing pages suggest. Across our testing, a free or trial tier handled all of the following without complaint:

  • Short, self-contained formats. Subject lines, captions, product blurbs, outreach emails. No context to carry.
  • Structural work. Turning notes into headings, reordering a draft, tightening a paragraph. Cheap for the tool and genuinely useful.
  • Testing whether a format suits you at all. The correct use of a free tier is to discover that you do not need a tool for this task.
  • Learning what a good brief looks like. Twenty bad drafts teach you more about specifying than any guide, and they cost nothing.

Where free breaks, in practice

The failure is rarely a hard stop. It is a slow tax: you start writing shorter prompts to save credits, you skip the second draft you would have asked for, and you stop experimenting. That behaviour change is the real cost of a ceiling, and it is invisible on a pricing page. The second break is context — if you write the same four document types every week, re-explaining the rules each session costs more than the subscription.

What you are actually paying for

Sorted by who genuinely gets value, rather than by price:

The low-cost dedicated writer — around $7.50 per month

Unlimited generation within a dedicated writing interface, with templates for the common formats. The model behind it is not the strongest, so drafts need more editing, but the volume ceiling disappears entirely. This is the right tier when your problem is how many drafts you produce rather than how good each one is — and for a solo writer producing ten pieces a week, that is usually the actual problem.

The general assistant — around $20 per month

A stronger model, and the ability to hold a long brief steady across a long document. It is the tier where editing time genuinely falls, not because the writing is prettier but because fewer things need restructuring. If your drafts come back needing to be rebuilt rather than edited, this is the wall you have hit.

The search-facing writer — around $20 per month

Adds search-oriented features on top of generation. Worth it only if your output is published on pages that have to be found, and wasted if you are writing emails and internal documents.

The team plan — around $49 per month

Multiple seats, shared brand voice and brief enforcement. The price only makes sense when several writers need the same constraints applied across volume. For one person it is a $40 premium on features you will not open.

Costs that appear after you commit

Three things are not on the pricing page and should be:

  • Editing time. Our 30-day benchmark measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant. At $30 per hour, the cheaper tool costs about $7 more of your time per piece — which erases the price difference fast if you publish often.
  • Annual versus monthly. Annual billing cuts the effective monthly rate materially on most of these tools. It is a good deal only if you are confident about the format you will be writing in twelve months.
  • The tool you keep paying for. Subscriptions are cancelled rarely. Before adding a second writing tool, check whether you used the first one this month.

How to decide in one afternoon

Pick a real document you write repeatedly. Produce it three times: once in the free tier, once on the cheapest paid plan, once on the stronger model. Time the editing on each, and count how much you had to restructure. The cheapest option that does not need rebuilding is your answer — and it is often not the one the pricing page pushes.

What we did not test

We did not test enterprise contracts, API pricing, or tools that bill per credit rather than per month, and per-credit billing behaves very differently under heavy use. Prices and limits here reflect plans available in October 2026 and move frequently; check the current page before subscribing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a free AI writing tool enough?

For short self-contained formats and occasional use, yes. You will feel it break when you start rationing drafts, or when you re-explain the same brief every session.

Which paid plan is worth it for one person?

Usually the low-cost unlimited writer if volume is the constraint, or the general assistant around $20 if your drafts come back needing restructuring rather than editing.

Is a $49 team plan worth it for a solo writer?

Rarely. Its value is shared voice and brief enforcement across several writers. Solo, you are paying for seats and governance you will not use.

Do free tiers allow commercial use?

It varies by tool and changes often. Check the current terms for the specific plan before publishing client work, rather than assuming from a general policy page.

What is the real cost difference between cheap and expensive tools?

Editing time. In our 30-day test the cheap tool needed about 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words against roughly 8 on the stronger model — around $7 of your time per piece at $30 per hour.
