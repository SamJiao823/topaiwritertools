The hardest part of a Substack isn't writing one good post — it's writing one good post every single week. Readers subscribe for a point of view, and they notice when the voice wobbles or the cadence slips. AI won't fix your voice, but it can remove most of the mechanical work so you have energy left for the parts only you can write. This guide covers the four tools we tested for 30 days, what each is best for, and where the human edit matters most.

1. The Real Challenge: Consistency Plus Voice

Two things decide whether a Substack grows: publishing on a schedule and sounding like one person. AI tools score well on the first and poorly on the second — if you let them. From our 30-day benchmark, Claude scored 9/10 on short-form, ChatGPT 8/10, Writesonic 8/10, and Rytr 7/10. Those scores measure draft quality, not voice. The draft is the easy part; the voice is your edit.

If you're new to the format, our how to write a newsletter with AI guide walks through the full workflow. And for the tool-by-tool breakdown, the AI tools for newsletters page compares the same shortlist from a general angle.

2. Use AI for Outlines, Headlines, and Structure

The fastest win for a weekly writer is delegating the decisions, not the sentences. Ask the AI for five potential headlines, an outline from your rough notes, and a stronger opening line for a draft you already wrote. These are judgment-lite tasks where a good model saves you 20 minutes.

You can do this with the free tier of Claude or ChatGPT. Paste your notes and ask: "Give me three newsletter structures for these points, and one headline each." Then pick the one that matches the episode you actually want to publish — the AI proposes, you decide.

3. Use AI for First-Pass Drafts, Then Reclaim the Key Lines

For a weekly essay, the fastest path is a first-pass draft from your outline, followed by rewriting the lines that carry your opinion. Let the AI handle the connective tissue: the transitions, the background, the context readers need. Then rewrite the thesis, the examples, and the punchlines yourself — those are the sentences readers screenshot and share.

This is where editing time matters. Claude averages ~8 minutes per piece in our tests, ChatGPT ~10 minutes, Writesonic ~12, and Rytr ~22. For a newsletter you publish weekly, that gap is real money — at $30/hr, Rytr's extra editing costs about $7 more per post.

4. The Four Tools, and When Each Is the Right Fit

Daily drafting, no budget: Claude at $0-20/mo (9/10 short-form) or ChatGPT at $0-20/mo (8/10 short-form). Both free tiers are strong enough for weekly work.

Discovery and growth: Writesonic at $20/mo (8/10 short-form) is the only budget tool with native SEO scoring in our benchmark. If you want your archive to rank for search and bring in subscribers who never saw your social posts, it's the best fit.

Tight budget, short snippets: Rytr at $7.50/mo (7/10 short-form) handles subject lines, teasers, and short posts well. Keep it away from long essays — its long-form score is 3/10.

You can try Claude, try ChatGPT, try Writesonic, or try Rytr directly. Full scoring is on the benchmark page.

5. The Human Edit That Makes It Sound Like You

Every AI draft carries tells: perfectly balanced sentences, a "furthermore" here, a tidy conclusion there. Your edit is what removes them. Concretely, in one pass: cut the first paragraph the AI wrote, tighten any sentence longer than 30 words, add one specific detail only you would know, and end on a question or a promise instead of a summary.

Our guide on making AI writing sound human lists the exact patterns to hunt for. And if you're ever unsure whether disclosure is worth it, the honest answer is that most readers assume you use tools anyway — the trust risk isn't the AI, it's the generic output.

6. A Weekly Workflow You Can Actually Keep

  1. Sunday, 30 minutes: Paste your week's notes into Claude or ChatGPT, get three structures, pick one.
  2. Monday, 20 minutes: Generate the first-pass draft from your chosen outline.
  3. Tuesday, 40 minutes: Rewrite the thesis, examples, and punchlines in your voice. Cut by a third.
  4. Wednesday, 15 minutes: Read it out loud, fix the rhythm, publish.

The tool keeps the schedule; the edit keeps the voice. That division is the whole trick — and it's why our how we test page treats editing time as a first-class metric rather than a footnote.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use AI to write my Substack newsletter?

Yes, if you treat it as a drafting partner. Use AI for outlines, headlines, and first-pass paragraphs, then rewrite the key lines in your own voice. The tools we tested score 8-9/10 for short-form, so the output is strong — but the voice still needs your edit.

What's the best AI tool for newsletters?

For daily work, ChatGPT and Claude free tiers are the best value at 8/10 and 9/10 short-form, with editing around 10 and 8 minutes per piece. Writesonic at $20/mo adds native SEO tools for discovery, and Rytr at $7.50/mo handles short snippets on a tight budget.

How do I make AI writing sound like me?

Feed the AI your past writing, edit every sentence that doesn't sound like you, and keep one specific personal detail per post. Our full guide on making AI writing sound human covers the exact patterns to remove — balanced sentences, filler words, and generic transitions.

Is AI content on Substack against the rules?

No, Substack doesn't ban AI-assisted writing. What readers punish is generic, unedited output, so the edit is the real quality gate. Disclosure is optional, but a growing number of writers note when AI helped — honesty tends to preserve trust better than pretending.
