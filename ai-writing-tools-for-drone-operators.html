A drone operator's writing has one unique property: some of it is read by a regulator. Licence class, registration number, maximum altitude, where you may and may not fly, insurance cover. Get those wrong in a marketing paragraph and a client repeats them to an authority. The rest is ordinary small-business writing, and that part the tools handle well.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Service page copy. Aerial photography, video, mapping, inspection, real-estate fly-throughs. Short factual descriptions repeated for every client.
  • Quote notes. Why the second flight costs more, what the per-hour rate covers, how the site size drives the price. Explaining the number is the whole job here.
  • Pre-flight and venue briefs. Where to park, who to contact on site, what time the light works, what happens if the wind picks up.
  • Post-delivery follow-ups. The link, the file format, the revision window, what a second round costs.
  • Social captions from one shoot. One flight becomes a caption, a package description and a client review request.
  • Equipment lists. Aircraft, batteries, lenses, what you bring to a standard job. Boring, identical, perfect for a model.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Licence and registration details. Writing that you are "fully licensed" without naming the class, or quoting a registration number that has expired. A client who repeats this is being careless; you are the one who published it.
  • Flight-envelope statements. "Fly up to 400 feet anywhere" is wrong in most jurisdictions and wrong near an airport. Altitude, line-of-sight and no-fly zones are facts, not copy.
  • Insurance summaries. Cover amount, whether it extends to the client's property or to a crew standing under a rotor. Insurance language needs the broker's wording.
  • Privacy promises. "We never film over neighbouring properties" when you have, or "all footage is destroyed after delivery" when you keep the raw files. Clients remember these sentences.
  • Accessibility and survey claims. Saying a mapping job produced measurements accurate to a given centimetre when you used a consumer camera. That is a data-quality claim.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test put editing at roughly 22 minutes per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer versus about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a solo operator the saving is the service page and the venue brief: the two documents that otherwise get rewritten badly from memory before every shoot.

A workflow that holds up

Write the compliance block once — licence class, registration, max altitude, insurance limits, privacy statement — with your broker and your licence document in front of you, and never generate that block from anything else. Everything else can come from a template: service page, quote note, venue brief. When writing a quote, generate the explanation first and then check it against what you actually charge, because the model will happily make the price sound generous. For the venue brief, leave the site specifics blank and fill them in the morning of the shoot. Read the service page the way a client on a phone does: if the licence question is not answered in the first two sentences, they will ask you by email, and that email is the one you least want.
