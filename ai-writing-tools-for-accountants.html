Accountancy writing splits cleanly into two halves, and only one of them belongs to a model. The first half is process — chasing documents, confirming deadlines, describing what a service includes. That half is repetitive, time-pressured and well suited to generation. The second half is anything with a figure or a rule attached, and that half has to come from your own files and your own review.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Deadline and document chasers. The third polite request for the same bank statement. Short, repeated dozens of times each season, and the one piece of correspondence nobody wants to compose at 9pm.
  • Service descriptions. Bookkeeping, payroll, VAT or sales-tax filing, year-end, advisory. Written once and reused on the website, the proposal and the onboarding pack.
  • Engagement scope documents. What is included, what is explicitly not, what happens if the client delivers late. Boring, protective, and exactly the kind of list a model handles consistently.
  • Onboarding packs. What to send, where to upload, who to contact, how long each step takes. Every practice has one and most are out of date.
  • Plain-language explanations. Describing a filing obligation or a deadline in words a client can act on, which is harder to write than the technical version.
  • Internal checklists. File review order, what to verify before sign-off. Internal, repetitive, and cheap to standardise.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Any figure. Thresholds, allowances, rates, deadlines measured in days. A model will state a number with complete confidence and no source. Every number in a client letter must come from your own working papers.
  • Rule statements that sound current. Whether something applies, to whom, and from when. Rules change and a model's knowledge does not. Anything rule-shaped gets checked against the current source.
  • Promises about outcomes. A sentence implying a refund amount, a penalty waiver or a likely result. Clients keep letters, and a generated implication reads like advice.
  • Scope creep written in. Generated engagement text that quietly includes work you did not price. The "not included" list is the section worth writing by hand.
  • Client confidentiality. Real figures, real names, real situations pasted into a tool without checking the retention terms. Read the data clause before the first paste, not after.
  • Filing deadlines inferred rather than looked up. A model does not know your jurisdiction's calendar for this year.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a practice the value is concentrated in one place: the chasing emails. They are the highest-volume, lowest-judgement writing an accountant does, and they are exactly what nobody has time for in season.

A workflow that holds up

Write one service description and one scope document properly, with the fee structure and the exclusions in them, then generate every variation from those two files. Keep a separate facts file per client with the figures you are allowed to use, so numbers are never typed into a prompt. Generate chasing emails from a small set of templates with the client's name, the missing item and the date as the only variables. Anything touching a rule, a threshold or a deadline gets looked up, not generated. Before a letter goes out, read it the way a client reads it — skimming for the one sentence about money — and check that sentence against the working papers.

Check the tool's retention and training terms first. A cheap plan with unclear data terms is not a bargain for a practice handling client financials.
