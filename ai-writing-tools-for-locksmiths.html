Where it clearly helped

Service page copy, at last written down. Most locksmith sites list services in one line each. Expanding each into what it covers, what it does not, what affects the price and how long it takes produced the pages that actually answered calls before they were made. Automotive, residential, commercial and safe work each got a page, and the "what affects the price" section did more to reduce disputes than anything else we wrote.

Quote follow-ups. A written quote that nobody replies to is the norm. A follow-up that restates the job, the price, the parts and the expiry date — and asks one direct question — converted noticeably better than a phone call we did not have time to make. Generated phrasing was fine here because there is nothing to invent: the details come from the quote.

Review requests. Asking once, at the right moment, with the specific job named. The template: what we did, when, and a direct request with the link. Generic requests produced generic reviews; naming the actual situation produced reviews that mention it, which is what a future customer reads.

Where it nearly went wrong

Price promises. The industry's real problem, and generated text makes it worse. Drafts write "affordable", "competitive pricing", "no hidden fees" and — worse — specific figures for a job that has not been seen. A locksmith price depends on time of day, travel, the lock, the mechanism and whether anything has to be destroyed. Publish the components of the price — call-out, labour, parts, out-of-hours — and let the customer see how it builds, rather than a number that will not survive contact with the job.

Anything about authorisation. Who is allowed to open this door, this vehicle, this safe. A draft will happily produce confident wording about what documentation is sufficient, and getting it wrong ranges from awkward to serious. Authorisation requirements go in from your own policy, stated plainly, and the written record of what you were shown stays yours.

Emergency response times. "We arrive in 20 minutes" reads well and is not a commitment you can keep across a city at 2am. Drafts add urgency language because urgency is the context. State how the queue works instead: what you can honestly say about prioritisation, and what you tell people on the phone.

The two calls that stay human

First, the price on the phone — quote the structure and the range, not a figure, and put it in writing before you drive. Second, anything to do with proving who someone is. Both are situations where a written sentence becomes the thing that gets argued about later, and both are short enough to write yourself.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a one-van operation the value is not speed but volume: service pages that did not exist before, and follow-ups that were not being sent at all.

A workflow that holds up

Write the service pages once, properly, with a "what affects the price" section on each — that is the section that prevents arguments. Send every quote in writing with its expiry, and follow up once with the details restated and one question. Ask for a review at the end of the job, naming what you did, and do it once rather than twice. Keep any sentence about authorisation, pricing commitments or response times out of generated text entirely, and read each public page as a customer in a hurry would: does it tell me what this will cost and how long it will take, honestly?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write locksmith service pages?

Yes, and it is the highest-value use — expanding one-line services into what is covered, what affects the price and how long it takes. Keep every price as a structure rather than a figure.

Should I publish prices?

Publish the components — call-out, labour, parts, out-of-hours — rather than a single number. Locksmith pricing depends on the lock, the time and travel, and a fixed figure will not survive the job.

What should never be generated?

Anything about authorisation or what documentation is sufficient, and any response-time commitment. Both become the thing that gets disputed.

Does it help with reviews?

With the asking, yes. A single request naming the specific job produces reviews that mention the situation, which is what a future customer reads.

What should a one-person operation set up first?

One service page per service type with explicit pricing factors, and a written quote template with an expiry date.
