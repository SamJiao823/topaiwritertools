Two document types carried most of the value: quote follow-ups and booking confirmations with clear arrival windows. A third — prohibited-items policy — is the one where generated text creates real exposure, because the rules vary by hauler, by facility and by jurisdiction.

The job is largely a scheduling and expectation business. Anything that reduces "when are you coming" and "how much will it actually be" is worth automating, and most of the documents in this trade exist to answer exactly those two questions.

Where it helped

Quote follow-ups

A customer who received a price two days ago and has not booked needs one specific nudge: the price, the slot still open, and a way to confirm in one tap. Generated versions were good at this once we removed the habit of restating the whole service description. Drafting fell from about 10 minutes to 3, and the shorter messages drew more replies than the detailed ones.

Booking confirmations

The confirmation that states the window, the crew size, what happens if access is blocked, and what payment methods are accepted. The highest-value line in the entire set is the arrival window with a call-ahead — it converts an all-day wait into twenty minutes that the customer can plan around. Generated text handled the structure well; the window itself must come from your actual schedule.

Review requests

Sent the same day, referencing the specific job rather than the generic service. We asked for wording that names what was collected and where, which produces reviews that mention the city and the service type — the two things that matter for local search. Timing beats wording here: same-day requests outperformed next-day ones regardless of phrasing.

Where it failed

Prohibited items lists

This is the serious one. Drafts produced lists of banned materials that read authoritative and were not checked against what your facility or municipality actually accepts. Paint, batteries, tyres, mattresses, propane tanks and anything containing asbestos are handled differently in different places, and a wrong list is both a safety issue and a liability. The fix is structural: keep the prohibited-items list as a maintained document owned by a named person, and have booking confirmations link to it rather than restate it. Generated text may summarise it; it may not originate it.

Volume estimates and pricing language

Drafts described pricing in confident terms — "a quarter truck load is about [X]" — with figures drawn from nowhere. Quote ranges must come from your own rate card. We moved all pricing phrasing to a template with variables so the model never invents a number.

Access and parking assumptions

Generated instructions assumed a driveway and street parking. For urban collections, that assumption fails and the job goes wrong at the kerb. Access is now a mandatory booking field — stairs, lift, distance from parking — collected before the crew is dispatched.

Donation and recycling claims

Text asserted that items would be donated or recycled. That is a claim about what happens after you leave, and it needs to match what your operation actually does. Where it is true, state it specifically; where it depends on condition, say so.

What customers actually check

Someone booking a collection is checking three things: how soon, how much, and whether you will take the specific item. A prohibited-items list that answers the third question directly converts better than any reassurance about being licensed or eco-friendly. In our testing, the pages and messages that named specific items — hot tubs, pianos, construction debris — drew more qualified enquiries than generic ones.

Worth adding: a clear statement on what happens if the volume differs from the estimate on site. It is the most common source of dispute, and having it written down beforehand resolves most of them.

Two more items pay for themselves. State the crew size and what it includes, because a customer who expects two people and gets one reads the price differently even when the price is identical. State the cancellation window in the confirmation rather than at the door — a fee discovered on the day produces a bad review, while the same fee stated at booking rarely produces a complaint.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Quote follow-ups went from about 10 minutes to 3, near $4 saved at $30 per hour. Booking confirmations dropped from around 14 minutes to 4. Review requests from 8 to 2. Policy documents showed no net saving — the verification cost matched the drafting cost.

A workflow that holds up

Template the booking confirmation with mandatory fields for the window, the crew and the access notes. Send the quote follow-up on a fixed schedule. Request reviews the same day, naming the job specifics. Prohibited items, pricing rates and recycling claims come from your own maintained documents — verified against your facility, and owned by a named person.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write our prohibited-items policy?

It can format one you already maintain. Do not let it originate the list — accepted materials vary by facility and jurisdiction.

Can it quote prices?

Only from your rate card, using template variables. Never let it produce a figure on its own.

What about volume estimates?

Collect the details at booking and confirm on site. A written statement of what happens if the volume differs prevents most disputes.

Are review requests worth automating?

Yes — same-day requests with job-specific wording noticeably outperformed generic next-day ones.

Should donation claims be in the confirmation?

Only if they match what your operation does, and with any condition stated.

Does this help a single-truck operation?

The booking confirmation alone removes most of the phone calls about arrival times.
