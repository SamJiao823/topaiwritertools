Everyone needs a bio, and almost no one enjoys writing one. That makes it a perfect AI task — with one trap: an AI bio written from a blank prompt sounds like every other AI bio. The fix is to feed the AI specifics before it writes a word, then add two details only you know. Here's the exact workflow, in three lengths, with a prompt you can copy.

1. Why Most AI Bios Sound the Same

Ask any AI for a bio and you get the same shape: "passionate about [topic], with a proven track record of [generic thing]." Recruiters, editors, and conference organizers see that dozens of times a day. The problem isn't the AI — it's that the bio contains nothing only you could write. It has no numbers, no specific result, no detail that another person couldn't copy-paste.

The fix is to force the specifics before the AI writes. That's what the fill-in-the-blank prompt in section 3 does. When the input is specific, the output stops sounding like a template — the quality of an AI bio scales with the quality of the details you give it.

2. The Three Length Formats

One bio doesn't fit every platform. Match the format to the place it lives, then draft all three in one session so the facts stay consistent across your profiles.

FormatLengthWhere it lives
One-liner1 sentenceTwitter/X, email signature, Slack
Paragraph bio3–5 sentencesLinkedIn, podcast guest pages, bylines
Full-page bio200–400 wordsAbout pages, speaker sites, press kits

The one-liner is the hardest, because you can't hide inside a story — you need a role, an audience, and one concrete result in a single sentence. Draft that one first, then expand it into the longer formats.

3. The Fill-in-the-Blank Prompt That Works

Instead of "write my bio," give the AI a template with your facts in it. Copy this prompt and replace the bracketed parts:

"Write a [one-line / paragraph / 300-word] bio for me in a calm, specific voice. My role: [title]. I help [audience] achieve [result]. My strongest measurable result: [number or fact]. What I did before: [one line]. One detail most people don't know: [detail]. Never use 'passionate,' 'excited,' or 'proven track record.'"

This works because every sentence the AI produces is anchored to something true. The role, the audience, the number, and the personal detail are all yours — the AI only arranges them. For more templates like this, see our collection of AI writing prompts.

4. The Two-Details Rule

After the AI draft, add exactly two things only you know: one specific number or year, and one off-script detail — the conference where you bombed, the client who paid late, the sport you play on weekends. Two details is enough; more than that reads as a brag list.

This is the same "human pass" rule we apply to all AI copy on this site. In our guide to making AI writing sound human, the core idea is identical: one or two concrete specifics beat twenty adjectives every time.

5. Kill the Clichés

Cut these on sight: "passionate about," "results-driven," "proven track record," "seasoned professional," "dynamic," "thought leader." Replace each one with a verb and a number: "wrote 40 grant proposals," "launched 12 email campaigns," "edited 300 articles."

A useful test: if a phrase could appear on anyone's bio, it shouldn't be on yours. If the AI draft contains a word you've seen on a hundred LinkedIn profiles, delete it and write the plain fact underneath.

6. Free AI Is Enough for a Bio

A bio is short-form copy, and the free tiers of the tools we tested handle it well. In our 30-day benchmark, ChatGPT's free tier scored 8/10 on short-form copy and Claude scored 9/10 — both free to start. You don't need a paid writer for a bio; you need the details, and those are yours to provide.

The paid tier only matters if you're writing dozens of bios for a team. For one personal bio, spend zero dollars and put the saved effort into the two details.

7. The Bio Editing Checklist

  • A number in the first two sentences — a result, a year, a volume.
  • Two details only you would know.
  • No "passionate about" or other generic adjectives.
  • Voice matches the platform — LinkedIn reads differently than a podcast page.
  • Reads aloud in under 30 seconds.

Run those five lines and the AI has done its real job: a fast draft you'd have dreaded writing from scratch, with the human details that make it yours. That's the difference between a bio that reads like a template and one people actually remember.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write a good bio for me?

Yes, if you feed it specifics. A bio AI writes from a blank prompt will sound generic; the same AI given your role, audience, and one measurable result will produce a draft you can use. Plan to edit it by adding two details only you know. The tool matters less than the facts.

How long should an AI-written bio be?

Match the length to the platform. One sentence for Twitter and email signatures, three to five sentences for LinkedIn, and up to 400 words for an About page or speaker site. Draft all three formats in one session so your details stay consistent. Short bios are harder than long ones.

What makes an AI bio sound like AI?

The tells are generic adjectives and rhythm. "Passionate about," "proven track record," and "results-driven" appear on most AI drafts. Sentences that all run the same length also read as machine-written. Fix both by adding a number, a specific verb, and two details only you would know.

Which AI tool is best for writing a bio?

The free tiers of ChatGPT and Claude are enough. ChatGPT scored 8/10 on short-form copy and Claude 9/10 in our 30-day benchmark, and both are free to start. Because a bio is short, paid features matter less than your input. Pick either and spend your time on the details.
