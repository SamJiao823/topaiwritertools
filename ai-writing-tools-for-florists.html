Floristry writing has one specific failure mode: the model knows the vocabulary but not the stems. It will write a beautiful description of a bouquet containing flowers that are out of season, in colours that do not exist together, at a size you cannot make for the price. The language is never the problem; the inventory is.

Where AI clearly helps

  • Product and bouquet descriptions. Once the stem list is fixed, the description writes itself — and rewriting fifty of them by hand is a Sunday nobody wants.
  • Care cards. Water, light, trimming, how long it should last. Short, factual, and identical for most arrangements.
  • Delivery confirmations and updates. When it left, who signed, what to do if nobody was home.
  • Occasion copy. Sympathy, congratulations, apology, new baby — each needs a different register, and that is a genuine strength of a model.
  • Wedding proposal structure. The sections, the questions to ask, the options, the timeline. Structure is the tedious half.
  • Seasonal and holiday pages. Valentine's, Mother's Day, the local event you serve every year.

Where it nearly got us in trouble

  • Flowers that are not in season. A peony bouquet described in November. Seasonal availability is the most common factual error in floristry copy.
  • Colours that do not exist. A "deep blue" rose, or a shade the dye process will not produce. Customers order the description.
  • Size implied but not stated. "Generous", "abundant", "statement" written into a listing at a price point that buys a modest bunch. Vague size adjectives cause the complaints.
  • Substitution and freshness promises. What happens when a stem is unavailable, and how long the arrangement is guaranteed to last. Those are your policies, not generated defaults.
  • Delivery windows. A generated "same day" that your driver cannot deliver on. Same-day is a promise, and it is the one customers rely on most.
  • The apology note written too smoothly. A generated apology reads polished and slightly evasive. That is the one message to write in your own words.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test put editing at roughly 22 minutes per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. For a florist the saving is concentrated in one job: rewriting the same fifty descriptions for each season, which is otherwise an entire evening.

A workflow that holds up

Keep a stem list per arrangement with what is in season, what colour it actually is and what size the price buys. Generate descriptions from that list, never from the arrangement name alone — the model will fill the gap with flowers it has read about. For care cards, write one and generate variations per flower type. Wedding proposals should be generated for structure only, with every number, date and stem filled in by you. When something goes wrong with an order, do not generate the apology; the polished version reads worse than an honest short one. Read each listing as a customer choosing in a hurry: if the size is not clear in the first line, that is the line to fix.
