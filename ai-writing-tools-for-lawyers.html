The Short Answer

The short answer: no AI tool writes ready-to-file legal documents — and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What does work is using Claude or ChatGPT for first drafts and plain-English rewrites, then doing the lawyer's job yourself: checking every citation, every fact, every clause. For the money, Rytr handles client-facing short copy (emails, intake messages) at a fraction of the cost. We break down which tool earns its keep on which task below.

The reality of AI in legal writing

Legal writing has two parts: the analysis (what you must verify) and the expression (how clearly it reads). AI tools are strong at the second and useless at the first. In our 30-day test, Claude produced the cleanest plain-English rewrites of dense clauses — the kind you'd send to a client who isn't a lawyer — but flagged nothing about whether the clause was correct.

The three tasks where AI consistently saved time: first-draft memos from research notes, client-facing email tone-downs, and standard-form intake communications. The three where it did not: final argument drafting, contract review, and anything touching confidentiality you're not allowed to paste into a public tool.

Rule we tested with and now live by: AI drafts, you own. Every AI output we generated went through a full human review pass before it was acceptable — that's the cost of doing this properly.

The privacy red line

Before anything else: never paste privileged client information into a consumer AI tool. Our 30-day test ran only on fictional data for this reason. If you need AI on real matters, the conversation starts with your firm's IT policy and a provider that offers a business/confidentiality tier — that's a procurement decision, not a writing-tools question.

What we tested (safely): anonymized first drafts, tone rewrites on public boilerplate, and memo structure. Everything below is reproducible on de-identified text.

The prompts that worked for us

  • Plain-English rewrite: 'Rewrite this clause in plain English a non-lawyer can follow. Keep every legal effect; cut jargon only.' — Claude handled this strongest in our tests.
  • Memo first draft: 'Turn these research notes into a first-draft memo: issue, rule, analysis, conclusion. Flag anything that reads like an assertion you can't back up.' — ChatGPT's free tier did this fine.
  • Intake email: 'Draft a short email to a new client confirming next steps. Warm but professional, under 150 words.' — Rytr handled this in seconds at $7.50/mo.

The through-line: every prompt we used asked the tool to flag what it's unsure about. That made the human review pass faster than starting from scratch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to use AI writing tools for legal documents?

Safe depends on the data. Never paste privileged or confidential client information into a consumer tool. For non-sensitive drafting and tone rewrites, AI is fine — but every output needs a full human review. Treat AI as a drafting assistant, never as the author of a filed document.

Which AI tool is a strong fit for lawyers writing client emails?

For short client-facing copy, a budget tool like Rytr ($7.50/mo) covers most intake and follow-up emails. For trickier tone work, Claude ($20) produced the most natural plain-English rewrites in our 30-day tests. Either way, edit before send.

Can AI write a legal research memo?

It can produce a well-structured first draft from your notes — issue, rule, analysis, conclusion — which saves significant time. But the analysis, citations, and conclusions must be verified by a lawyer. AI is a structure and expression tool, not a legal research engine.

Do AI tools understand legal jargon?

They handle it better than most expected — Claude in particular rewrote dense clauses into clear language well. But understanding jargon and knowing the law are different. The tools never flagged substantive errors in our tests; that check is yours.
