What does Grammarly actually do — and what it refuses to do?

Grammarly is a proofreader with opinions. It checks spelling, grammar, and punctuation (the correctness layer), then moves up the ladder to clarity (wordy or confusing sentences), engagement (flat or repetitive phrasing), and delivery (tone). The free tier handles the correctness layer well; Premium unlocks the higher layers and full-sentence rewrites.

The thing to get straight before you start: Grammarly does not write articles for you. If you sit down expecting it to draft a blog post from a prompt, you'll be disappointed. Its AI assistant can draft short passages and rewrite sentences, but it's built to polish work that already exists. That makes it a great companion to a generator like Jasper or ChatGPT — and an odd purchase if you only want a generator. If that's you, skip to the bottom where we talk about when you shouldn't buy it.

How do I install the Grammarly browser extension?

The extension is the whole ballgame. Skip it and you're using Grammarly in one place instead of everywhere. Setup takes about two minutes:

  1. Go to grammarly.com and create a free account with an email or Google login.
  2. Install the extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari from the download page.
  3. Pin the Grammarly icon to your toolbar so you can see when it's active.
  4. Click the icon, sign in, and enable the extension. It'll ask you to confirm access to your browsing data — that's how it reads text fields.
  5. Open Gmail or Google Docs and start typing. Color-coded underlines appear as you go.

It works in plain text boxes too — Twitter/X, CRM fields, Slack — anywhere there's a typing box. One honest caveat: it deliberately stays out of password fields and some sensitive forms, so don't be surprised when it goes quiet there. How do I use Grammarly to edit AI-generated content?

This is the workflow we found most valuable across the whole 30-day test — and the reason Premium paid for itself in week one. AI drafts are fluent but repetitive; Grammarly's job is to make them sound like a person wrote them.

  1. Generate your draft in ChatGPT, Jasper, or wherever, and paste it into the Grammarly web editor at app.grammarly.com.
  2. Fix red first. Correctness issues — spelling, grammar, punctuation — are non-negotiable. Click each underlined word and accept.
  3. Then blue. Clarity suggestions are where AI text gets tightened. You'll see a lot of these; AI loves filler ("in order to", "it is important to note that").
  4. Then green and purple. Engagement and tone fixes smooth out the flat, encyclopedic voice AI defaults to.
  5. Read the result out loud and only then publish. Grammarly gets the floor clean; it doesn't make the writing good.

Our numbers from the test: editing an AI-generated 1,500-word article went from roughly 30 minutes down to about 18 — a ~40% cut. Most of those minutes came from blue and purple suggestions, not typos. Worth saying plainly: accept suggestions selectively. Grammarly's rewrites occasionally flatten the voice you actually want. On our ten-article test set, we rejected about a third of the rewrite suggestions.

How do I use Grammarly's tone and clarity suggestions?

In the web editor, click any underlined word and a panel opens with a plain-English explanation of what's wrong and a one-click fix. The "View all suggestions" panel on the right shows the whole document's issues at a glance, sorted by severity — that's the fastest way to sweep a long draft.

The tone detector is the underrated feature. Before you send a message or email, click the Grammarly icon to see how your text reads — "confident", "formal", "neutral", and so on — plus a slider to nudge it. We used this constantly for client-facing emails and cold outreach. In the editor you can also set goals (audience, formality, domain) so suggestions adapt to what you're writing, which matters more than people think: writing for a medical blog and writing for Reddit get very different suggestion sets.

How do I use the Grammarly AI writing assistant?

The assistant lives in the web editor's right-hand panel. It can rewrite a selected passage, shorten it, expand it, or change its tone on command. Ask it to "rewrite this more formally" or "cut this to three sentences" and it does exactly that, in place, with the same underline-and-accept flow as everything else.

Honest verdict after 30 days: treat it as a rewrite tool, not a research tool. Its best work is fixing and reframing your own sentences. Its worst work is generating new content from scratch — it drifts generic, and you still have to fact-check everything. We compared it against ChatGPT side-by-side on repurposing tasks and the quality was comparable, which is fine when you're already inside Grammarly and don't want to leave the document. How do I use Grammarly in Word, Google Docs, and the desktop app?

  • Word: The plugin adds a Grammarly sidebar inside Word for Windows and Mac. It works, though the suggestions render in the sidebar rather than inline, which makes batch-fixing slower than the web editor. On Mac, if you hit plugin issues, just use the web editor instead.
  • Google Docs: With the browser extension installed, Docs gets a "Grammarly" tab on the right. This is our default writing setup — extension in Docs plus a final sweep in the web editor.
  • Web editor: app.grammarly.com is the long-form workhorse. Paste anything in, get the full panel, goals, and the AI assistant. No plugin required, works on any machine.
  • Desktop app: It reads text on your screen across any app — great for Outlook or desktop email where extensions don't reach. Heavier than the extension, so we only used it for email-heavy days.

How do I use Grammarly with a team?

Teams get the most value from the Business plan's style guide, not its AI. Set a style guide once — preferred spellings, banned words, tone rules — and everyone on the team gets the same suggestions. Brand tones let you save a voice profile for each client or publication and switch between them per document. Snippets are a small bonus for boilerplate that you'd otherwise type every time (address blocks, sign-offs, standard disclaimers).

The realistic take: if your team mostly writes emails and short copy, the free tier plus one shared style doc is 90% of the value. Upgrade to Business when you're enforcing brand rules across several writers at volume — the style guide only matters if people actually act on it.

When should you skip Grammarly?

Let's be honest about the cases where it's not the right buy:

  • Your writing is already clean and your budget is tight. If grammar issues are rare in your drafts, Premium's $12/month buys little. Use the free tier or skip it entirely.
  • You need a generator, not an editor. Grammarly won't draft your articles. For long-form generation, Jasper or the budget pick Rytr do the writing, and then Grammarly free can clean it up.
  • Your problem is research or facts. Grammarly never touches accuracy. It polishes the surface; if your content is wrong, it's wrong in better English.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Grammarly worth it in 2026?

Yes, if you regularly edit writing that isn't yours — AI drafts, freelancers, teammates. In our 30-day test, Premium paid for itself within the first week. Skip it if your own writing is already clean and your budget is tight.

Does Grammarly work with AI-generated content?

Yes — editing AI drafts is Grammarly's single biggest use case. It doesn't detect AI writing; it improves it, catching the repetitive phrasing and wordiness common in generated text.

Is there a free version of Grammarly?

Yes. The free tier covers spelling, grammar, and punctuation, plus basic tone detection and limited clarity suggestions. Premium is roughly $12/month billed annually.

What's the difference between Grammarly Free and Premium?

Free handles the correctness layer. Premium adds full-sentence rewrites, clarity rewrites, tone adjustments, fluency, vocabulary suggestions, and plagiarism detection.

Does Grammarly improve your writing or just your score?

It catches real errors, but you have to accept suggestions with judgment. Its rewrites occasionally flatten your voice, so it works best as a filter, not an auto-approve tool.
