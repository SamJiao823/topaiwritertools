Follow-Up Email Templates: Twelve Ways to Chase Without Nagging

Most follow-ups fail because they ask again without adding anything. Each template below adds one new thing — a date, a document, a decision, a deadline — which is what makes a second email worth the reader's time rather than a reminder that you exist.

A follow-up has to carry new information, or it is just noise with a polite opening. "Just checking in" asks the reader to do work they already declined to do, and gives them nothing to act on. Every template here is built around something that changed since the last email: a date passed, a document exists, a number moved, a decision is needed by Friday.

The twelve

Replace every [bracket]. If a template still reads well with all the brackets empty, it is saying nothing.

1. The added-value follow-up

Use when: no reply to a proposal, and you have something genuinely relevant.

Sent you [thing] on [date]. This came out since and relates to the [objective] you mentioned: [link or one-sentence summary]. No action needed — thought it was relevant.

2. The deadline follow-up

Use when: there is a real date, and it is not invented.

Flagging a date rather than chasing a reply: [thing] closes / pricing changes / the slot opens on [date]. If it is a no, say so and I will stop — that is a fine answer.

3. The easy no

Use when: several attempts have gone quiet.

Last one from me. If this is not a fit, reply with a single word — "no" is genuinely useful and I will close the file. If it is a timing thing, tell me a month and I will come back then.

4. The invoice, first reminder

Use when: an invoice is a few days late.

Invoice [number] for [amount] was due [date]. Attaching it again in case it was buried. Let me know if there is an approval step I should be routing this through instead.

5. The invoice, second reminder

Use when: two weeks late.

Following up on invoice [number], [amount], now [days] past due. Can you confirm it is in the payment run, and give me a date? If there is a problem with the work, tell me directly and we will sort that out first.

6. The invoice, final notice

Use when: you need to escalate, and you mean it.

Invoice [number] is [days] overdue. If I do not hear from you by [date], I will [specific, real consequence: pause work, hand to collections, refer to the contract]. I would rather not — call me and we can arrange something.

7. The post-interview thank you

Use when: within 24 hours of an interview.

Thanks for the time today. The [specific topic discussed] was the useful part for me — it clarified [what you now understand about the role]. That is the area I would start on, and here is how I would approach the first [period].

8. The interview status check

Use when: the stated timeline has passed.

You mentioned a decision around [date]. I know these slip. Still interested — is there anything else you need from me, and is there a revised timeline?

9. The proposal revive

Use when: a proposal went quiet weeks ago.

The [proposal] from [date] assumed [scope and price]. Two things have changed on my side since: [change]. If the scope is different now, send me the new version of the problem and I will reprice rather than re-send.

10. The meeting recap

Use when: you promised to follow up, or nobody wrote anything down.

What I took from today: [decision one], [decision two], [open question]. I am doing [action] by [date]. Correct me on any of it — the recap is only useful if it matches what everyone else heard.

11. The quiet client

Use when: a current client has gone silent mid-project.

Work on [project] is paused at [specific point], pending [thing I need from you]. Nothing is blocked on my side except that. If priorities have moved, tell me and I will requeue it rather than chase.

12. The re-engagement, months later

Use when: restarting after a long gap.

We last spoke in [month] about [thing]. Short version of what happened since on my side: [one or two sentences of real change]. If [their situation] has changed too, worth ten minutes — if not, no need to reply.

What to cut from any draft

Generated follow-ups reliably add four things, and all four reduce the reply rate:

  • "Just checking in" / "circling back" / "touching base." It announces that you have nothing new. Delete the phrase and lead with the new thing.
  • "As per my last email." Correct, and it puts the reader on the defensive. Forward the thread instead.
  • A restatement of your whole pitch. They have read it. One sentence of context, maximum.
  • Apologising twice. One "sorry to chase" signals politeness. Two signals that you know you are wasting their time.

Three follow-ups to write by hand

Some chases carry consequences, and a generated version will get the tone wrong in a way that costs you:

  • Final notice before escalation. Only send it if the consequence named is real and you intend to act. Write it yourself, in plain sentences.
  • Anything about a disputed invoice. Where money is contested, the wording becomes evidence. Short, factual, dated.
  • A relationship worth keeping. If the person matters beyond this deal, the email should sound like you, because they will remember it.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test put a first follow-up at roughly 6 minutes with a low-cost dedicated writer and about 4 minutes with a general assistant — the saving is in phrasing, not judgement. You still have to decide whether anything has actually changed since the last email, and if nothing has, the right move is to wait rather than send.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many times should I follow up?

Four attempts is a reasonable ceiling for sales outreach and two for a job application. After that, send one "easy no" and close the file.

How long should I wait before following up?

Five to seven working days for a proposal, three to four for cold outreach, two days after an invoice due date, and 24 hours for a post-interview thank you.

Is "just checking in" bad?

It signals you have nothing new to add. Lead with whatever changed — a date, a document, a number — or wait until something does.

Should follow-up emails be short?

Shorter than the original email. The reader has context; they need the new information and one clear question.

What if they never reply?

Take it as a no and stop. One final message offering an easy no gets a surprising number of replies and lets you close the loop cleanly.
