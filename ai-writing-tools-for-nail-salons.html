Writing the service menu

A menu that converts names three things per service: what it includes, roughly how long, and the price range. Generated text will write "Deluxe Pedicure" with no anchor; you should add the time and what makes it deluxe, because that is what lets a client self-select and reduces no-shows from mismatched expectations.

Booking replies

The structure that works answers: next available slot, what to arrive with (bare nails if removing old product), and the deposit or cancellation rule. Customers comparing salons remember who made booking effortless, and a clear cancellation line protects your chair time without sounding harsh.

Where they failed

Chemical safety. Nail products contain substances that need ventilation and correct handling, and some ingredients are restricted or banned in certain regions. Do not let generated text claim a product is "safe" or prescribe handling. State that products are used per manufacturer guidance and the applicable local requirements, and defer any health claim to qualified sources.

Licensing and sanitation. Who may perform which service, and the sanitation steps required between clients, are set by local regulation and differ by place. State that services are provided by licensed staff following the applicable sanitation requirements, naming the relevant authority to confirm with rather than asserting a standard.

Medical-adjacent claims. "Strengthens nails" or "repairs damage" drift into claims a generator cannot back. Keep descriptions to what the service does cosmetically and defer any therapeutic claim to appropriate professionals.

Aftercare honesty

Every finish behaves a certain way. State the realistic care — how long before water, what shortens wear, what to avoid — in the aftercare note rather than the handover chat. Writing it down is what prevents the "it chipped in a day" complaint that was actually normal wear. The note also sets the expectation that some edge lifting is normal, which stops it reading as a failure of the service.

The retail add-on wording

A salon's writing does quiet selling every time a client is in the chair, and the posts that fill the diary often pair a service with a product. The wording that works names the benefit, not the pitch: "these cuticle oils are what keep the set looking fresh between visits" reads as care, while "buy this today" reads as a transaction. We kept product mentions to a single sentence attached to the service they support, never a standalone block.

The booking confirmation is the other quiet seller. A confirmation that names the appointment, the arrival tip — bare nails if removing old product — and one gentle retail nudge ("if you want [product] on the day, reply and we will set it aside") converted better than a separate promo email and annoyed no one. The rule is restraint: one nudge per message, attached to something useful, never a second ask. Clients who get a clear, single-nudge confirmation are also the ones who rebook, because the next step was already obvious.

What clients read first: the price, how long, and whether they need to bring or remove anything. Lead with those three. The retail and the warmth belong lower, and the line that most reduced no-shows was stating clearly whether removal of old product was included or a separate step. Clients who know that up front do not ring the day before to ask.

Editing time, from our 30-day benchmark

Menus, booking replies and social posts: 2–4 minutes. Anything touching chemical handling, licensing or therapeutic claims: 6–10 minutes, all of it replacing confident assertions with correct deference to the applicable rules and qualified sources. That time is not optional in this trade.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI writing tools handle my salon menu?

Yes, for structure and wording. The tool cannot know your actual services, times and prices, so supply those; the layout is then fast to produce.

How should I write about product safety?

As use per manufacturer guidance and the applicable local requirements, never as a "safe" claim. Defer health claims to qualified sources rather than asserting them.

What about licensing and sanitation?

State services are provided by licensed staff following the applicable sanitation requirements, and name the relevant authority to confirm with. Do not assert a single standard that varies by location.

Can I claim a product strengthens nails?

Keep descriptions to the cosmetic effect and avoid therapeutic claims a generator cannot support. Defer any health-related claim to appropriate professionals.

What reduces aftercare complaints?

Writing the realistic care — water wait, wear shorteners, what to avoid — into the aftercare note, not just saying it at handover. Expectations then match the result.
