Newsletter Templates: 8 Structures for Emails People Keep Opening

A newsletter fails in one of two ways: nothing in it was worth opening, or everything in it was a link. Both are structural problems. These eight templates each have a reason to exist before the links start.

The unsubscribe usually happens weeks before the click that causes it. Someone opens, skims, finds a list of links they did not ask for, and files you under "later" — then unsubscribes when the next one arrives. The fix is not better writing, it is giving every issue something that stands on its own, even if the reader clicks nothing.

Before you pick a template

Decide one thing: what is this email for, on its own? To teach one thing, to make one offer, to ask one question. A newsletter that is purely a contents page has delegated its value to the links, and readers notice. Also settle the ratio: how much is written here versus how much is behind a link. Writers who move more of the value into the email tend to keep subscribers longer, even though it feels like giving things away.

The eight templates

1. The digest

Use when: several small things happened and none deserves its own email.
Subject: [Month]'s [number] things: [most interesting one]
Structure: one-line intro, then three to five items — each with a headline, two sentences of why it matters, and one link. Put the strongest item second, because people skim past the first.

2. The single idea

Use when: you have one insight. This is the highest-performing structure and the hardest to write.
Subject: [The claim, stated]
Structure: claim in the first sentence (no preamble), the reasoning in three short paragraphs, the counter-case in one, then a closer. Internal links only where they genuinely add something.

3. The teardown

Use when: you can show your reasoning on something concrete.
Subject: What we changed about [thing], and what it cost
Structure: before state, what triggered the change, what you did, what it cost in time or money, what happened, what you would do differently.

4. The question

Use when: you will actually read the replies. This is also research.
Subject: Can I ask you something about [topic]?
Structure: one question, why you are asking, what you currently believe, and an explicit invitation to disagree. Reply to everyone, and report back next issue.

5. The roundup

Use when: you are curating rather than originating.
Subject: [Number] things worth your time ([topic] edition)
Structure: each item gets one sentence of description and one of your opinion. Without the opinion you are a feed.

6. Behind the scenes

Use when: the process is more interesting than the result.
Subject: How [result] actually happened
Structure: the starting assumption, the thing that broke, one specific detail nobody sees, the outcome, what is next.

7. The announcement

Use when: you are launching something. Send it separately from your regular issue.
Subject: [Thing] is live — and here is who it is not for
Structure: what it is in one sentence, who it suits, who it does not, price, how to start. The "not for" paragraph prevents the wrong signups.

8. The re-engagement

Use when: someone has not opened in months. Send once, then let them go.
Subject: Should I keep emailing you?
Structure: honest framing, what they have missed in two lines, one click to stay, one to go — and actually remove them. Suppressing non-openers protects your deliverability with everyone else.

Subject lines that survive the inbox

Across our testing, the patterns that held up were concrete over clever. A named thing beats an adjective — "The $7 writing tool that survived our test" outperforms "Our amazing tool findings". A question works if you will answer it, and reads as clickbait if you will not. Numbers in subject lines help when the number is specific: odd numbers read as real, round ones read as marketing. Keep it under about 45 characters so the front half survives on mobile. And never write a subject line you cannot deliver on in the first two sentences — that mismatch is what teaches people to stop opening your mail.

What this costs, from our 30-day benchmark

Our 30-day test measured roughly 22 minutes of editing per 1,000 words on the low-cost dedicated writer against about 8 minutes on the general assistant — near $7 per piece of your time at $30 per hour. Newsletters sit in the middle: long enough that coherence matters, short enough that the cheap tool copes if you supply the structure.

A workflow that holds up

Pick the format a week ahead and keep a running notes file — the half-ideas, the screenshot, the line someone said. Most of a good newsletter is written before you sit down, just not organised. Generate from real material, not from the topic alone; a tool given only "write about pricing" will produce something true and forgettable. Then do the two edits that matter: cut the opening preamble — start with the claim, not with "this week I have been thinking about" — and remove every sentence that exists to transition. Finally, read it once on a phone, because that is where it will be read.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a newsletter link out or contain the content?

Mostly contain it. Issues that deliver value inside the email keep subscribers longer; a pure list of links trains readers to skim and eventually unsubscribe.

How long should each issue be?

Long enough for its format. Single-idea emails run 300 to 600 words; digests can be shorter if each item earns its place.

Can AI write a whole newsletter?

It can shape one from material you supply. Given nothing but a topic it produces something true and forgettable — the specifics are what make it yours.

What should I do about non-openers?

Send one honest re-engagement email, then suppress the people who ignore it. Protecting deliverability matters more than list size.
